## THE QUESTION

[VISUAL: Prompt text on screen]

Here's the question. 2025, Long Essay Question 2: "In the period circa 1200 to 1450, Buddhism, Hinduism, and Confucianism included ideas about social structures, gender roles, and political authority that influenced societies across Asia. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which one or more of these belief systems shaped societies and/or political systems in Asia during this period."

Two things matter. First: "the extent to which" means you're arguing how much these beliefs actually shaped societies — not just listing facts. Second: "one or more." You can go deep on one.

The rubric is six points, and every point is earned independently.

[VISUAL: Rubric graphic — six slots: Thesis (1) / Context (1) / Evidence (2) / Analysis & Complexity (2)]

Knowing the facts unlocks maybe half of them. Let's grade.

## LIVE GRADING — ESSAY C: 1/6

[VISUAL: Essay C on screen, score counter at 0/6]

This essay is one paragraph, about sixty words. Thesis: Confucianism heavily shaped Asian political systems because of its ideas about social hierarchy — children below adults, that kind of thing. barely there — but it's a claim plus a reason, and that counts.

[VISUAL: Checkmark — THESIS: 1 point. Counter: 1/6]

And that's it. Context? The second sentence just says China followed strict social hierarchies — a vague restatement of the prompt, not a description of any actual historical development. No. Specific evidence? None — no dynasty, no policy, no named concept. Analysis? Nothing. Final score: one out of six.

[VISUAL: Big final score — 1/6]

The diagnosis: this student treated the LEQ like a short answer. One claim, zero follow-through. The thesis is the door into the rubric, not the house. Write only a thesis, and you are choosing a one.

## LIVE GRADING — ESSAY B: 4/6

[VISUAL: Essay B on screen, score counter reset to 0/6]

This student clearly studied. Thesis: Confucianism played a crucial role in shaping the societies and political systems of China, Korea, and Vietnam, woven into state bureaucracy and social norms. Claim plus reason.

[VISUAL: Checkmark — THESIS: 1 point. Counter: 1/6]

Evidence: filial piety. The Song and Ming dynasties promoting Confucian values through education and the civil service exams. The Mandate of Heaven. Two full points — because the student put the facts to work. Filial piety anchored family life. The exam system trained officials in Confucian principles, embedding them in government. The Mandate legitimized emperors based on their ability to govern. Facts that carry an argument earn both evidence points; a list of facts earns one.

[VISUAL: Checkmarks — EVIDENCE: 2 points. Counter: 3/6. ANALYSIS: 1 point. Counter: 4/6]

Analysis earns a point through cause and effect — this belief led to that outcome. The two it lost: contextualization, never attempted. The essay never zooms out to the broader picture — the spread of Buddhism and Hinduism across Asia, or new states rising in Southeast Asia. And complexity: one track the whole way — "Confucianism shaped East Asia." No comparison, no second effect, no tension.

[VISUAL: Two empty rubric slots — CONTEXT: 0, COMPLEXITY: 0. Final score: 4/6]

The painful one, because the student knew the material. The gap between this and a six isn't more studying — it's two structural moves never written: a context sentence and a more layered argument.

## LIVE GRADING — ESSAY A: 6/6

[VISUAL: Essay A on screen — long essay, scroll indicator]

Thesis: these belief systems shaped societies through social hierarchies, gender roles, and the maintenance of power. Three categories up front — the grader knows exactly where the essay is going.

[VISUAL: Checkmark — THESIS. Counter: 1/6]

Context: a full paragraph on the Song Dynasty's promotion of Neo-Confucianism, explaining why Confucianism carried renewed political weight in this period. A real described backdrop, not a passing phrase.

[VISUAL: Checkmark — CONTEXT. Counter: 2/6]

Evidence is where this essay feasts. On Hinduism: the caste system by name, plus reincarnation and moksha, each tied to how the system held social order in place. On Confucianism: the hierarchy of relationships, the Mandate of Heaven, the civil service exams. And the evidence works for a living. Reincarnation taught people their rulers deserved respect — high rank proved good past lives — so belief maintained political power. The civil service exam gave lower-class families a shot at advancement, keeping them invested instead of resentful — cementing the people already in charge.

[VISUAL: Checkmarks — EVIDENCE: 2 points. Counter: 4/6. ANALYSIS: 1 point. Counter: 5/6]

Complexity: a multi-pronged argument from many specific pieces of evidence — including the layered point that the exam helped the lower classes and protected the elite at the same time. Six out of six.

[VISUAL: Checkmark — COMPLEXITY. Counter: 6/6. Final score: 6/6]

But here's the twist I opened with. The last paragraph — labeled "Complexity," comparing Asian religions to the Spanish Inquisition — wasn't a relevant or insightful connection, per College Board. It earned nothing. The point was already won by the body paragraphs.

[VISUAL: The labeled "Complexity" paragraph highlighted in red — "earned 0"]

Tattoo this on your brain: complexity is not a paragraph. It's a property of your whole argument. You can't bolt it on at the end with a random comparison — and you don't need to, if your argument already has layers.

## THE MISS PATTERN

[VISUAL: Score ladder — "1 = thesis only" / "4 = facts without framing" / "6 = framed + layered"]

The pattern? Students don't bleed LEQ points through wrong facts. They bleed them through moves they never make. The one-point essay had no specific evidence. The four-point essay had strong evidence but skipped context and stayed on one note. The six-point essay framed the context and layered the argument.

The two most-skipped points are always the same pair: contextualization — a real described backdrop, not a one-phrase mention — and complexity — your argument having more than one gear: a comparison, a second effect, a tension, a layer.

## THE TAKEAWAY RULE

[VISUAL: Text card — "Facts get you to a 4. The moves get you to a 6."]

The rule: facts get you to a four; the moves get you to a six. You're not just answering the question — you're writing the rubric. One sentence of real context. A thesis with a reason built in. Evidence connected to an argument. An argument with more than one angle. Every point needs its own move, written on purpose. Do that, and the points stack up by themselves.

## YOUR EXAM — THE 2027 FORMAT BRIDGE

[VISUAL: "2025: choose 1 of 3 LEQ prompts" → "2027: ONE LEQ prompt, no choice"]

One thing to know. In 2025 you chose one of three LEQ prompts — so students picked the period they knew best. On your exam, May 2027, that choice is gone. One broad prompt. No dodging a period or theme you don't like.

The good news: the rubric is exactly the same — same six points, same moves. Everything you just watched still applies; you just have to be ready for any topic. For reference: the short-answer questions are all required now with set source types, and the document-based question spans 1200 to 2001.
