# We Graded 3 Real AP Essays — Here's Why the "Right Answer" Got a 4

**Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes (~1,000 words of narration)

**Source:** Question from the 2024 AP World History exam, released by College Board.

---

[VISUAL: Cold-open title card. Split screen: "4/6 — the textbook answer" vs. "6/6 — the messy one."]

Here's a grading result that should make you uncomfortable. On this 2024 LEQ, one student wrote the "textbook" answer — the Mandate of Heaven in China, the Divine Right of Kings in Europe — and scored a 4 out of 6. Another student wrote a sprawling, messy essay about Ottoman taxes, Mughal buildings, and the Mongols — and scored a perfect 6.

These are real essays, scored by College Board. I'll grade three of them live against the 6-point rubric, showing you the exact sentence where each point was won or lost.

[VISUAL: The prompt on screen, highlighted.]

The 2024 prompt reads: "In the period circa 1200–1750, religious traditions were used to establish and legitimize rulers and governments throughout Afro-Eurasia. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which religion was the main source of political legitimacy for rulers in Afro-Eurasia during this period."

Two words are doing all the work here: "evaluates the extent." This isn't "list ways rulers used religion." It's "make an argument about HOW MUCH religion was the main source." That argument gets graded on six points: thesis, contextualization, two for evidence, two for analysis and reasoning. Every point is earned by a specific move. Let's grade.

[VISUAL: Essay 1 excerpt on screen with a 6-row rubric checklist; checkmarks animate in.]

Essay one is long and a bit scattered — taxes, architecture, janissaries, the Safavids, all crammed together. But its thesis line works: it says rulers used religion to legitimize their rule "because religion helped with tax collection, and because religion influenced strict laws." A real claim with reasons — an argument to test, not a reworded prompt. Thesis: earned.

Contextualization: in the intro, the student describes Islam spreading across Afro-Eurasia from 1200 to 1750, with missionaries carrying it along trade routes. That's a real, relevant bigger-picture process, actually described. Context: earned.

Evidence, worth two points. First point for naming specific, relevant facts — the jizya tax, the janissaries, Sunni Ottomans and Mughals versus Shi'a Safavids. Second point because the evidence actually supports an argument: the jizya paragraph explains the tax was "a constant reminder that the empire was Islam," and rulers could set it however high they wanted — proof religion was doing political work. Both evidence points: earned.

Analysis: the essay uses comparison — Ottoman versus Mughal taxation, Safavid strict enforcement — to structure its argument. First reasoning point: earned.

And then the complexity point — the move that separates 5s from 6s. In paragraph two, the student flips the script: the Mongols, unlike every other empire in the essay, built legitimacy on conquest, peace, and trade rather than religious conformity. The Mongols "were able to control and facilitate peace and trade throughout their empire without a heavy reliance or influence on religion" — and the argument stops being one-note. A genuine counterexample, doing real analytical work. Complexity: earned. Total: six out of six. Notice: not because it was beautiful. Because every point is a move, and this student made all six.

[VISUAL: Essay 2 excerpt; rubric checklist — two rows turn red.]

Essay two covers exactly what your textbook says: the Mandate of Heaven, the Divine Right of Kings. Sounds safe, right? The thesis works: it claims religion was a main source of legitimacy "through the Mandate of Heaven in Chinese dynasties, and the Divine Right of Kings in European states." Defensible, with two clear categories. Thesis: earned.

Then contextualization — and this is where the first point bleeds. The essay opens: "Throughout history, rulers have always had to legitimize their power to the people, as a failure to do so could lead to rebellion or a weakened state." College Board's verdict: too vague. There is no actual historical event, development, or process described — it's a general observation about all of history. It could sit on top of any prompt ever written. Context: lost.

Evidence: both points earned — the Mandate of Heaven cycle and the Divine Right of Kings each get specific treatment tied to the argument.

Analysis: the essay directly compares the Mandate of Heaven and Divine Right. Historical reasoning: earned. But complexity? No attempt. The essay never qualifies, never brings a third region, never asks "but what about rulers who did the opposite?" Complexity: lost. Total: four out of six. This is the painful one — the facts were all there. Two missing moves cost two points.

[VISUAL: Essay 3 excerpt; rubric checklist — thesis, context, complexity turn red.]

Essay three shows the most dangerous pattern in the set. Its thesis attempt: "Religion was extremely important and one of the main sources of political legitimacy for rulers in Afro-Eurasia as seen through different states and empires." That's the prompt wearing a costume. No specific empires, no methods, no line of reasoning. Thesis: lost.

Context: "Throughout the period circa 1200–1750, there were many different sources for political legitimacy... One process, however, showed to be very important." Again — describing nothing in particular. Context: lost.

Evidence: the student knows the Divine Right theory, the pope crowning kings, China's dynastic cycle — both evidence points earned. But watch what happens next: the essay drifts into human sacrifice among the Aztecs and Incas. Interesting — and completely outside Afro-Eurasia. On this question, out-of-region evidence doesn't earn credit. The valid examples carried the evidence points, but every out-of-region paragraph was wasted writing time.

Analysis: one comparison of Divine Right in two places earns the first reasoning point. Complexity: never attempted. Total: three out of six.

[VISUAL: "The three bleeds" graphic — three bullet points animate in.]

Same three bleeds across all three essays. First: a thesis that restates the prompt instead of arguing it. If your thesis doesn't name a reason or categories, it's decoration. Second: context that could fit any essay ever written. Name a real process — the spread of Islam, the Wars of Religion — and actually describe it. Third: the complexity point sits unattempted in two of three essays. One move — a counterexample, a regional comparison, a "but it wasn't that simple" paragraph — wins it.

[VISUAL: Takeaway rule on screen.]

Here's the rule. Grade yourself one point at a time. Before you write, map six moves: a thesis with a reason, one described historical context, two connected pieces of evidence, one comparison or change-over-time structure, and one paragraph that makes your argument more complicated. Write for the points, not for the topic.

[VISUAL: "On YOUR exam — May 2027" card.]

One format note. This question is from 2024, when students chose one of three LEQ prompts. On your exam in May 2027, there's no choice: the LEQ is one single broad prompt covering the whole course. You can't dodge a period or a theme. The rubric — the six points — is exactly the same. So everything you just watched still applies. It just applies to a prompt you can't escape. Prepare for every period, and make every move.
