## COLD OPEN — HOOK (0:00–0:40)

Here's something that catches almost every student off guard: the essay that scored a perfect 6 out of 6 on this question doesn't say the Mongols were great. It says they helped spread a plague that killed millions of people.

Meanwhile, the essay that scored a 1 out of 6 — one point — didn't get a single fact wrong. It just never argued anything.

We're going to grade three real student essays, live, against the six-point LEQ rubric, using College Board's own scoring commentary. By the end, you'll see exactly where points are earned and lost — and why the difference between a 4 and a 6 has almost nothing to do with how much history you know.

## THE QUESTION (0:40–1:20)

[VISUAL: prompt text on screen]

This is Long Essay Question 2 from the 2023 AP World History exam. The prompt:

"In the period circa 1200 to 1450, the expansion of empires such as the Mongol Empire facilitated trade and communication across Eurasia. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which Mongol expansion affected the peoples of Eurasia during this period."

## WHAT THE PROMPT IS REALLY ASKING (1:20–2:10)

Notice what it's NOT asking. It's not "tell me facts about the Mongols." The key words are *evaluates the extent to which*. Extent means: how much? In what ways? For whom? Was it a big deal for everyone, or only some peoples — and did it help them or hurt them?

So this question tests continuity and change: did the Mongols change Eurasia, or did life mostly continue as before? The six points are thesis, contextualization, two evidence points, and two analysis points — and every point is earned independently. You can win evidence without winning thesis. Remember that, because it matters for what we're about to see.

## LIVE GRADING — ESSAY 1: THE 6/6 (2:10–4:10)

[VISUAL: essay 1 intro paragraph on screen, thesis sentence highlighted]

Essay one scores 6 out of 6. Watch exactly where each point lands.

**Thesis — point.** The very last sentence of the intro: "Mongol expansion affected the peoples of Eurasia during the period 1200–1450 by spreading religion, spreading technology, and spreading disease." Three categories: religion, tech, disease. That's a thesis with a line of reasoning — it tells the reader the entire structure of the essay in one sentence.

**Contextualization — point.** Right before it, the student describes the Silk Roads thriving under Mongol protection, drawing merchants because the roads were safe. That's not random background — it connects the Mongols to a bigger development, long-distance trade, that frames the whole question.

**Evidence — two points.** And here's why: the student doesn't just name-drop. They give Samarkand and Karakorum as trading cities, missionaries spreading Islam and Christianity, Mongol religious tolerance — then the compass, the astrolabe, flying cash, banking techniques. Every example is specific, and every one is attached to the argument: *this is how the Mongols moved culture*.

**Analysis — one point.** The student does the reasoning: all this "set the stage for the age of exploration." That's causation — how Mongol trade led to a later development.

**Complexity — the sixth point, and the one most students miss.** This student spends a whole paragraph on the downside: the same Silk Roads spread the bubonic plague, which depleted Europe's workforce and helped break feudalism apart. Connection AND catastrophe. Positive and negative. That's the nuance the rubric means by "complex understanding." Six for six.

## LIVE GRADING — ESSAY 2: THE 4/6 (4:10–5:40)

[VISUAL: essay 2 paragraph 3 on screen, the inaccurate sentence highlighted]

Essay two scores 4 out of 6. The two lost points are the most instructive thing in this video.

**Thesis — point, but barely.** "They did this through their reintroduction on the Silk Roads trading route and their religious tolerance." A claim with two categories. Fine, point earned.

**Contextualization — point.** The Silk Roads had fallen out of use before the Mongols; the Mongols started as nomads. Relevant context, point earned.

**Evidence — only one of two.** The religious tolerance material works: Buddhism spreading into China as the Mongols absorbed new peoples. Specific, linked to the argument. But then this sentence: that the Mongols spread "mathematic ideas from India to the rest of the world." College Board calls that one out as historically inaccurate. One wrong example — and the second evidence point is gone. Not "almost earned." Gone.

**Analysis — one point.** The student does explain how conquest fostered cultural exchange. But **complexity — zero.** The entire essay is "the Mongols helped." There's no but, no downside, no second look. College Board's words: "no attempt to demonstrate a complex understanding." That's the second lost point.

## LIVE GRADING — ESSAY 3: THE 1/6 (5:40–6:30)

[VISUAL: essay 3 full text on screen, opening sentence annotated "overgeneralized"]

Essay three scores 1 out of 6. This one stings, because the facts are fine. The student mentions the Silk Roads and religious tolerance — and that earns the single evidence point.

But here's College Board's verdict on the rest: the evidence was "not linked to a supporting argument." The student never makes a claim and never explains how the Mongols changed anything. The opening is a vague wave — "the Mongols started to conquer more lands... it had a lot of effects for Eurasia" — which CB calls overgeneralized, so no contextualization point. No line of reasoning, no reasoning, no analysis points. One point total.

The lesson: knowing things is not the same as arguing things.

## THE MISS PATTERN (6:30–7:10)

[VISUAL: three bullet points appearing one by one]

Three ways this question bleeds points — and they're the same three on almost every LEQ:

**One: floating evidence.** The 1-point essay had facts but no argument for them to work in. On the LEQ, a fact without a job earns you nothing. Evidence points require evidence *supporting an argument* — that's the actual wording in the rubric.

**Two: accuracy matters more than you think.** The 4-point essay lost its second evidence point on one inaccurate sentence. If you're not sure a claim is right, leave it out. A wrong example doesn't just fail to help — it costs you.

**Three: one-sided essays die at 4.** The 6-point essay wins its last two points by arguing both directions — connection and catastrophe. Complexity is just a fancy word for "yes, but."

## THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE (7:10–7:30)

If you take one rule into your LEQ: **two pieces of evidence tied to one argument beat five facts floating free.** And build your thesis as categories that include a counterweight — the best single sentence you can write is one that argues both sides at once, like "spreading religion, spreading technology, and spreading disease."
