# We Graded a Real DBQ: Industrialization & Women (2025 Set 2)

- **Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes
- **Narration word count target:** ~900–1,100 words
- **Attribution:** Question from the 2025 AP World History exam, released by College Board.

You can write one of the better essays in the whole batch — four documents used correctly, a clear argument — and still walk away with a 4 out of 7. That's exactly what happened to one essay we're grading today. The painful part? It didn't lose points on anything hard. It lost the points students leave on the table every year. Let me show you.

---

## [THE QUESTION]

[VISUAL: The prompt on screen, one line at a time]

The question asks you to evaluate the extent to which the spread of industrialization gave women new opportunities, new challenges, or both, between roughly 1850 and 1950. That's it — one sentence.

Now, here's what it's really asking. "Evaluate the extent" is not "list facts about factories." It wants you to weigh: were the opportunities bigger than the challenges, or the other way around — and for whom? A Russian factory organizer and a Chinese laborer didn't experience the same industrialization, and your essay has to notice that.

---

## [LIVE GRADING — ESSAY ONE: THE 4 OUT OF 7]

[VISUAL: Rubric checklist on screen. Each point lights up green or red as we grade.]

**Thesis — earned.** The writer opens with a claim that has two sides: some women gained "new leadership opportunities in their communities," while others faced "low wages" and "poor working conditions." It answers the prompt and gives the essay direction. Green check.

**Contextualization — earned.** The intro describes the Industrial Revolution starting in Britain with coal and steam, then spreading worldwide. That's a real, relevant bigger picture. Green check.

**Evidence from the documents — both points earned.** This is where the writer shines. For opportunities: a memoir by a Russian labor organizer describing women elected to lead factory workers' societies, and a 1912 Russian government report showing women taking over village councils as men moved into factory jobs. For challenges: an Ottoman postcard of a silk factory in 1903 — crowded floor, child labor, a male supervisor watching — and a Japanese activist's memoir of being recruited into a silk factory at age twelve for twelve-hour days. Four documents, all accurate, all tied to the argument. Two points. The essay sits at 4.

And then the points start leaking.

**Evidence beyond the documents — lost.** Not a single fact in this essay comes from outside the seven documents. One whole point gone — and one outside example would have earned it.

**Sourcing — lost.** Read the whole essay and you'll notice something: not once does the writer tell you WHO wrote a document or WHY it matters. The Russian memoir is treated like a neutral camera. The Japanese activist's memoir is treated like a weather report. But documents aren't neutral — they were written by people with motives, and the rubric rewards you for noticing. This writer never tries. Zero attempts, zero points.

**Complexity — lost.** Complexity almost always rides on top of sourcing and outside evidence. With neither attempted here, there's nothing to push the argument deeper. Lost.

Final tally: four out of seven. And notice — the writer didn't lose a single point on the things students stress about most: thesis, context, evidence. All three lost points are the analysis points.

## [LIVE GRADING — ESSAY TWO: THE PERFECT 7]

[VISUAL: New essay on screen. Scorecard resets to 0/7.]

Now let's grade the essay that got everything right. Thesis, context, and evidence — earned the same way, using four documents including the Japanese silk-factory memoir and a Black South African educator's account of women moving into cities as domestic workers when their land was taken.

Here's where it separates. **Evidence beyond the documents — earned.** The writer brings in the Triangle Shirtwaist factory fire — an event from outside the packet — tying it to factory owners who cut corners on women's safety for profit. One fact, one connection, one point.

**Sourcing — earned**, on four documents. This is the masterclass. The writer doesn't just say what the South African labor activist wrote — the writer says WHY she wrote it: to convince women that factory hardships were still better than being trapped in domestic life. The writer notes the Chinese activist, writing in exile, had a motive: pushing women toward communism. Every sourcing move ends with the same thing — a link back to the argument. It's not trivia about the author; it's the author doing work for the thesis.

**Complexity — earned.** Sourcing on four documents, multiple perspectives woven together, outside evidence throughout — the essay is genuinely thinking, not just listing. Seven out of seven.

## [THE MISS PATTERN]

[VISUAL: Side-by-side scorecards. 1B: 4/7, red on the three analysis rows. 1A: 7/7.]

So what does this question reveal about how students bleed points? Look at the two essays side by side. Same evidence skill. Same solid thesis and context. The entire gap — three points — is the analysis layer: sourcing, outside evidence, complexity. The worst-earned components on the whole exam were sourcing at 0.39 out of 1, evidence beyond the documents at 0.47, and complexity at a brutal 0.15 out of 1. The first essay is a perfect portrait of that data: a good writer who described documents beautifully and never once asked "who wrote this and why?"

One more caution — a third essay in the batch earned thesis and context but used only two documents. You need at least three just to describe, and four to actually argue. It lost both evidence points on a mechanical mistake. Count your documents before you put the pen down.

---

## [THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE]

[VISUAL: Rule card on screen, one sentence.]

That's the one rule to tattoo on your brain for the DBQ:

**Every document has an author with a motive. Name the motive, and connect it to your argument.**

That's sourcing, and it's the cheapest point on the exam. One sentence per document: "As a labor activist trying to rally women to her cause, she emphasizes the factory's cruelty" — who she is, why she writes, how that serves YOUR point. Describing what a document shows is not sourcing. Sourcing is explaining why it exists the way it does — and why that strengthens your argument.

The first essay we graded never did that once. It cost the writer the sourcing point AND the complexity point, which almost always dies with it. The perfect essay did it four times.
