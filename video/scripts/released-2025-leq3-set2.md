# "We Graded This": The LEQ Where Perfect Facts Still Lost 2 Points

**Source:** Question from the 2025 AP World History exam, released by College Board.
**Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes (~1,000 words of narration)

---

[VISUAL: Title card — "WE GRADED THIS" stamp over a handwritten LEQ essay]

**Cold open:**

You can earn every single evidence point on the LEQ and still walk out with a 4. College Board gave full evidence marks to an essay that named John Locke, quoted "life, liberty, and property," and pointed at the U.S. Constitution. The facts were fine. Then: zero points for analysis. Not for wrong history — for a missing sentence.

[VISUAL: Scoreboard graphic — 6/6, 4/6, 2/6 with each essay's opening lines]

Here's the uncomfortable truth: the LEQ doesn't pay for facts. It pays for the sentence that connects the fact to your argument. Let's grade three real 2025 answers, live, and find every point that was won — and the ones that quietly slipped away.

[VISUAL: Prompt text on screen]

**The question:**

"During the eighteenth century, Enlightenment philosophers developed new ideas about individual rights and the role of governments. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which Enlightenment ideas encouraged movements for political change and/or social reform during the period circa 1750 to 1900."

**What it's really asking:**

The job is in the last clause: "evaluate the EXTENT to which Enlightenment ideas encouraged movements." That word — extent — means you're not listing revolutions. You're taking a position: how much credit do Enlightenment ideas actually deserve? That's a causation argument. The six-point rubric: one point for a thesis that actually argues something, one for historical context, two for evidence, and two for analysis — one for reasoning that structures your argument, one for showing complex understanding.

[VISUAL: 6-box rubric diagram — Thesis, Contextualization, Evidence ×2, Analysis ×2]

**Essay 3A — the one that scored 6 out of 6.**

This student opens with a claim: that with the influence of these new ideas, movements that would never have happened without the Enlightenment began — "such as the American Revolution, the Haitian Revolution, and the women's rights and suffrage movement."

[VISUAL: Essay 3A intro with thesis sentence highlighted]

Why does that earn the thesis point? It's not a restatement of the prompt — it gives the argument's categories: two political revolutions and one social movement. That's a line of reasoning.

Then context: the Enlightenment as a challenge to knowledge dictated by the Church and to unquestioned authority — Locke, Montesquieu. That connects the topic to a bigger picture: a shift in how people thought about authority itself. One point.

Evidence: the Declaration of Independence, Locke's natural rights in Jefferson's justification, Montesquieu's separation of powers, Louverture and the Haitian Revolution, Wollstonecraft and women's suffrage — multiple examples, each tied to the argument. Two points.

Now watch the analysis. Every body paragraph runs a cause-and-effect chain: Locke's natural rights gave Jefferson the language to call British rule a violation of rights; Montesquieu's ideas shaped the separation of powers; Enlightenment language helped a Haitian slave revolt grow into a revolution that also ended slavery. That's reasoning structuring the argument — point one. Complexity came from the nuance: the Enlightenment used to justify a revolution AND to design the government that followed; the Haitian Revolution starting as a slave revolt and becoming something broader. Plus the Wollstonecraft move — activists pointing out the irony of governments built on Enlightenment ideals while denying women the vote. That's a qualifier on "extent." 6 out of 6.

[VISUAL: Essay 3A with checkmarks filling all six rubric boxes]

**Essay 3B — 4 out of 6. The one that had the facts and lost anyway.**

The thesis earns its point: the essay claims Enlightenment ideas created movements and rebellions "in hope of freedom for many colonies" between 1750 and 1900. A position plus a timeframe. Point.

Contextualization: the opening names Locke and natural rights — "life, liberty, and the pursuit of property." Just enough to count. Point.

Evidence: the U.S. Constitution, Locke, natural rights — accurate, specific, tied to the argument that Enlightenment ideas shaped political change. Two points.

So why 4, not 6? The middle of the essay says many colonies rebelled because they "wanted to become an independent state." College Board's commentary is blunt: the essay "does not explain any connection between rebellion and Enlightenment ideas." The student named the Enlightenment. The student named rebellions. But it never wrote the sentence linking them — never explained HOW the ideas caused anyone to rebel. Both analysis points gone. Facts that sit in the same paragraph as an argument don't earn reasoning. The connecting sentence IS the reasoning.

[VISUAL: Essay 3B with four green checks, two red X's on both Analysis boxes]

**Essay 3C — 2 out of 6. The painful one.**

This one earns its thesis point — and not where you'd expect. The opening paragraph's thesis attempt is historically off, crediting "social darwinism and marxism" with causing the French Revolution, dated 1896. But the thesis point can live in the introduction OR the conclusion, and the last paragraph claims that as the Enlightenment spread globally, it inspired oppressed nations to revolt and reform. That's defensible, so the point lands. Thesis point one.

But context: the opening waves vaguely at colonialism, saying philosophies "began reaching people subject to these kinds of powers." That's not a described historical development — it's a hand wave. Zero.

Evidence gets one point, for the Haitian Revolution tied to natural rights. But the second point is gone: the French Revolution paragraph is never connected to the Enlightenment — and the social darwinism claim is what College Board calls a "read-through error," an inaccuracy graders just read past. Only one piece of evidence ever gets used to support an argument. And with no causation, no comparison, no structured reasoning anywhere, both analysis points vanish. Two out of six — for facts that were real but never built into an argument, surrounded by history that was invented and counted for nothing.

[VISUAL: Essay 3C with one thesis check, one evidence check, red X's everywhere else]

**The miss pattern:**

Two misses. Miss one: evidence without the connecting sentence. Essay 3B proves it — you can name Locke and the Constitution and still score zero analysis points if you never say HOW the idea caused the change. The rubric treats "these rebellions happened" and "the Enlightenment happened" as two unrelated facts until you write the sentence joining them.

[VISUAL: Miss-pattern card — "Miss 1: Facts without the connecting sentence" / "Miss 2: Invented or vague history earns nothing"]

Miss two: invented or vague history earns nothing. Essay 3C's dates were off by a century, its causes were fabricated, and its context was a shrug at colonialism. On the LEQ, an inaccurate claim isn't a half-point — it's a read-through. And vagueness is no better: context has to describe a real development, not gesture at one.

**The one takeaway rule:**

Every example gets a "because" sentence. Name your fact, then write the sentence that connects it to your argument: HOW did this Enlightenment idea cause this movement? Evidence + Because = Reasoning. If your paragraph could survive deleting every sentence after the facts, you're writing Essay 3B.

[VISUAL: Formula card — "Evidence + BECAUSE = Reasoning"]

**On your exam (May 2027) — the format bridge:**

In 2025, students chose one of three LEQ prompts — you could dodge a period you didn't like. On your exam, that's gone: a single broad prompt. No choice, no dodge. You can't pick the question that fits your favorite facts, so arguing with whatever you're given is the whole game. The six-point rubric is unchanged.

So: take a position on the "extent," give it context, connect every fact to your argument with a because-sentence, and add one layer of depth. That's the 6. Go earn it.
