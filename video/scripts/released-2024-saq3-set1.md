Here's a question where two students wrote the same answer — "the Columbian Exchange" — and the graders gave one the point and the other nothing. The official scoring notes explain the difference, and it comes down to one missing sentence.

This is "We Graded This": real student responses from a released exam, graded live, point by point, so you can see exactly where points die.

Today's question is about European colonization of the Americas, 1450 to 1750. Let's put it on screen.

## THE QUESTION ON SCREEN

[VISUAL: The three-part question displayed cleanly, each part appearing in sequence: (a), (b), (c).]

Part (a): identify one method Europeans used to expand their empires in the Americas. Part (b): explain one way colonialism affected Indigenous peoples. Part (c): explain one way interactions with non-Europeans in the Americas helped build a global economy. Three parts, three points, each graded on its own.

Now notice the verbs — they're the whole game. "Identify" means name one specific thing. "Explain" is where this question gets dangerous: it means show the *how*. Not just name the thing. Connect it.

## LIVE GRADING — STUDENT ONE: 3 OUT OF 3

[VISUAL: Handwritten student response 3A on screen, styled like an exam booklet page. Each part gets a green checkmark as it's graded.]

Student one. Watch how fast this earns all three points.

For part (a), the student writes — quote — "One method was forced labor systems," naming the hacienda and the mita, where Indigenous people were forced to work, expanding the empire. One named, specific method. Done. One for one.

Part (b): the student says Europeans spread smallpox, causing, quote, "the Great Dying, the eradication of most Indigenous people." Disease named, effect stated. Two for two.

Part (c) is the one that separates students, and this student nails it. Quote: "Spanish conquerors forced non-Europeans to mine silver in places like Potosí, Bolivia," then sold the silver to China in exchange for luxury goods like silk — showing, quote, "the global economic flow of silver." See the chain? Interaction: forced mining. Mechanism: silver flows across the Pacific to China. Result: a global economy forming. Three for three — the graders' notes say exactly this.

Three sentences per part. Full credit. Remember that.

## LIVE GRADING — STUDENT TWO: 2 OUT OF 3

[VISUAL: Handwritten student response 3B on screen. Green checks for (a) and (b); part (c) gets a red X.]

Student two wrote more words than student one. Got fewer points. Here's why.

Part (a) earned the point — the student names colonization: claiming land, building shelter and agriculture. The graders accepted it: a method of expanding an empire. Vague, but it counts.

Part (b) earned it too — Indigenous peoples lost their lands and homes, and were killed by European diseases. Two points.

Now part (c). This is where it falls apart. The student writes that European interactions with non-Europeans "developed their global economy" because Europeans "learned how to work with the soil" and "found indigenous weapons and learned how to make them."

[VISUAL: Part (c) text isolated and highlighted; the lines about soil and weapons get red underlines.]

The graders' verdict: that doesn't clearly show an interaction between Europeans and non-Europeans — and there's no explanation of how it built a global economy. "Interactions developed the global economy" is a claim, not an explanation. No mechanism. No trade route, no silver, no goods crossing an ocean. The point is gone.

They knew things — they just never said *how*. Zero for part (c).

## LIVE GRADING — STUDENT THREE: 1 OUT OF 3

[VISUAL: Handwritten student response 3C on screen. Red X on (a), green check on (b), red X on (c).]

Student three is the painful one, because the knowledge is clearly right there.

Part (a): the student answers "the Columbian Exchange" — it let Europeans convert Indigenous peoples and trade crops like maize. Sounds smart. Gets zero. The question asked for a method of *expanding empires* — conquering or controlling territory. The Columbian Exchange is a process of exchange, not a method of expansion. The graders say it flatly: not a method used to expand empires. Wrong question answered.

Part (b) earns the point: smallpox brought by Europeans killed more than half the Indigenous population, and others were taken into servitude. Disease, effect, done.

Part (c): "The Columbian Exchange contributed to the global economy because... new crops introduced... weaponry and material... influence Asia and the production of rice... new crops as well as new animals." A wall of true-sounding words — zero points. The graders: this "does not explain how the Columbian Exchange contributed to the creation of a global economy." Listing things that moved isn't explaining how a global economy developed. Crops moved — so what? No point.

One out of three. And the cruel part: this student clearly studied the Columbian Exchange — and deployed it in two parts where it couldn't earn.

## THE MISS PATTERN

[VISUAL: Two-column graphic. Left column: "THE MISREAD." Right column: "NAMING vs. EXPLAINING."]

So what's the pattern? This is how students bleed points on "explain" questions.

First, the misread: part (a) asked for a method of *expansion*, and "Columbian Exchange" — big, famous, wrong — answered a different question. Before you write, circle the verb and the target: what exactly is being asked for?

Second — the big one — naming instead of explaining. "The Columbian Exchange contributed to the global economy" is a label, not an explanation. College Board's bar for "explain" is: show me the *how*. Silver mined in Potosí, shipped across the Pacific, buying Chinese silk — that's a global economy forming, mechanism visible. "New crops were introduced" is a fact floating in space. Student two did the same thing in different clothes: "interactions developed the global economy" is a claim wearing an explanation's outfit.

## THE TAKEAWAY RULE

[VISUAL: The rule appears on screen, one line at a time: "Name the thing → say what it caused → connect it to the question."]

So here's the rule. Take it into the exam with you: every "explain" part needs a because-chain. Name the thing, then say what it caused, then connect that cause to what the part is asking about. Three links. If you read your answer back and you can't find the "which caused" in the middle, the point isn't there yet. Write the mechanism.

Identify: name it. Explain: show the how. That's the whole rubric.

## ON YOUR EXAM — MAY 2027 FORMAT BRIDGE

[VISUAL: Side-by-side comparison graphic: "2024: SAQ 3 or SAQ 4 — your choice, no stimulus" vs. "2027: Q1, Q2, Q3 — all required. Q3 = map, chart, or image."]

One last thing — the format bridge. This is a 2024 question: no stimulus, and you chose SAQ 3 or SAQ 4. On your exam, May 2027, there's no choice — all three SAQs are required, and each has a mandated source: Q1 a secondary text, Q2 a primary text, Q3 *always* a non-text source — a map, a chart, or an image.

On your exam, a question like this would likely come with a map of silver trade routes or a chart of Indigenous population collapse — and part (c) might ask you to use that visual. But the skills don't change. Identify still means name it. Explain still means show the how. Same rubric bones — just one more piece of evidence to work with.
