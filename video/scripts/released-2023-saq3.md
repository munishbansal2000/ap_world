# We Graded This: 2023 SAQ 3 — The Question Where Describing Slavery Earns You Zero

**Question from the 2023 AP World History exam, released by College Board.**
**Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes (~950 words of narration)

---

## COLD OPEN

[VISUAL: Scorecard on screen — "Part A: 1 point. Part B: 0 points. Part C: 1 point."]

Here's a student who knew their stuff. On this question, they wrote a real answer about African traditions surviving in the Americas — religions, dances, languages carried across the ocean. Sounds good, right?

It earned zero points.

Not because it was wrong. Not because it was poorly written. But because it answered a question College Board didn't ask. And that single habit — answering the question you *wish* they'd asked instead of the one they did — is the most expensive habit in short-answer land.

Today we're grading three real student responses to this question, live, against College Board's own scoring notes. Watch for the trap — because part B is where this question takes your points.

## THE QUESTION

[VISUAL: Prompt text on screen]

The question has three parts, all about the Atlantic slave trade, roughly 1450 to 1750:

Part A: **Identify** one reason for the increase in enslaved people being moved across the Atlantic.

Part B: **Explain** one way the growing number of enslaved people caused social or cultural change in the Americas.

Part C: **Explain** one way the demand for enslaved people affected African populations.

Notice the verbs. A says "identify" — that's just naming a cause. B and C say "explain" — that means you need a how or a why, not just a fact.

The skill being tested here is causation: cause, effect on the Americas, effect on Africa. Each point is earned independently — so even if you blow one part, you can still grab the other two.

## SAMPLE ONE: THE PERFECT SCORE

[VISUAL: Sample A response, clean formatting, labeled "Score: 3/3"]

Let's start with the one that nailed it.

For part A, this student names the rise of the plantation system in the Americas — and then gets specific. Sugar in Brazil and Barbados, tobacco and rice in the southern colonies. And crucially, they explain *why the demand existed*: native laborers had died off from disease, they knew the land well enough to escape, and there were moral arguments against enslaving them — they even name the critic who made those arguments. That's identification with real content behind it. One point.

[VISUAL: Highlight "rise of the plantation system" + plantation specifics]

Part B: the student says enslaved Africans brought over their own religions, dances, and languages, which survived and blended into American culture — naming one African-derived religion by name. The key word in the prompt is "cultural change." The student shows a change: a culture previously dominated by European traditions absorbing African ones. One point.

[VISUAL: Highlight the religion name + "cultural change" link]

Part C: the student explains that because most enslaved people taken were young men, African societies were left with a gender imbalance — a disproportionate number of women, which even reshaped marriage practices toward polygamy. That is a specific effect *on African populations*, with a mechanism. One point.

Three for three. But notice what this student did in every part: they didn't just drop a term. They gave the *mechanism* — the how and the why.

## SAMPLE TWO: THE PART-B WIPEOUT

[VISUAL: Sample B response, labeled "Score: 2/3 — where did the point go?"]

Now watch how easily a good answer goes sideways.

Part A: this student identifies that Native Americans were dying of disease, creating a labor shortage Europeans filled with enslaved Africans. That's a valid reason. One point earned — even though the writing is rough. College Board explicitly says grammar doesn't count against you.

Part C: the student explains high death rates on the slave ships and brutal conditions — a defensible effect on African populations. One point earned.

But part B? Here's what they wrote: "when so many Native Americans died to disease, they just disregarded the number lost and replaced them with more people as if nothing happened."

[VISUAL: Sample B part B text highlighted in red, with CB commentary quote: "not a social change that resulted from increasing numbers of enslaved peoples"]

That earned zero. And here's the diagnosis: the student answered part A *again*. Native Americans dying of disease is a cause of the slave trade — it's the answer to part A's question, not part B's. Part B asks for a *social or cultural change in the Americas caused by enslaved people arriving*. Casta system, syncretic religions, new social classes — that's the target. This student described the *circumstances of enslavement* instead. That's the trap of this question: writing about how bad slavery was is not the same as explaining a social change.

## SAMPLE THREE: THE MISSED SWEEP

[VISUAL: Sample C response, labeled "Score: 1/3"]

This one hurts, because the student clearly knew content.

Part A: "people in the western hemisphere needed workers for different types of labor." That's a valid reason — demand for labor. One point.

Part B: the student writes that enslaved people became property and weren't given human rights by their owners.

[VISUAL: Sample C part B highlighted, CB commentary: "identifies the circumstances of enslaved peoples, but does not explain how a specific social change resulted"]

Zero points. Same disease as Sample B: this describes the *condition* of enslaved people, not a *change in society or culture* caused by their arrival. Being treated as property is a circumstance. A casta system, new vocabulary for mixed-race families, African religions blending with Christianity — those are social changes. The answer never lands on one.

Part C: the student claims European diseases wiped out African communities when Europeans came to get slaves.

[VISUAL: Sample C part C highlighted, CB commentary: "incorrectly explains that the population decline in Africa was due to the spread of new diseases"]

Zero points — and this time it's a straight-up accuracy miss. The population impact was enslavement and removal, not European disease in Africa. That inverts the actual causal story.

Final score: 1 out of 3.

## THE MISS PATTERN

So what's the pattern? Look at the two lost points on part B across Samples B and C. Neither student was wrong about history. Both described something *true*. But both described the *wrong category of answer*.

College Board's scoring notes spell it out: part B demands a specific social or cultural *change* that *resulted from* the growing enslaved population. When you're under time pressure, your brain reaches for the strongest thing you know about the topic — and for slavery, the strongest thing you know is probably "it was brutal" or "people died of disease." But the question doesn't want your strongest feeling. It wants the category it asked for: a *change*.

The tell that you're about to bleed a point: your answer to part B could also answer part A. If it explains *why* the slave trade grew, it's an A answer, not a B answer. Part B must answer: *what changed in the Americas because enslaved people were there?*

## THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE

[VISUAL: Rule card on screen]

Here's your rule, and it's worth a full point on any SAQ like this:

**"Describe" a circumstance loses. "Explain" a change wins. If your sentence could move to a different part of the question without breaking, it's in the wrong place.**

Before you move on from any part, check it against the verb and the category — cause, effect, change. Each part is its own locked room. An answer that belongs in part A earns nothing in part B, no matter how true it is.

## ON YOUR EXAM (MAY 2027)

[VISUAL: "2023 vs 2027" format comparison graphic]

One important note. This question comes from 2023, when students got to *choose* between two no-stimulus questions. On your exam, that choice is gone.

In the new Fall 2026 format, all three SAQs are required — no choosing — and each has a mandated source type. SAQ 1 always pairs with a secondary text. SAQ 2 always pairs with a primary text. And SAQ 3 — the question you just watched — *always* comes with a non-text source: a map, a chart, or an image. You will never face a bare, no-stimulus Q3 again.

What does that change for you? The reasoning skill doesn't move — causation is causation. But you now have to *read the image or chart first* and ground your answer in it. When your Q3 arrives, look at the map or chart, pull one concrete fact out of it, and let that fact anchor your answer. The students you just watched answered from memory alone. On your exam, the source hands you the evidence — use it.
