# We Graded This: The Nazi Poster Question (2024 SAQ 2) — Why "Explain" Costs Students a Point

**Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes
**Narration:** ~1,000 words
**Source:** Question from the 2024 AP World History exam, released by College Board. Student responses and scoring rationale from College Board's official scoring commentary.

---

[VISUAL: the 1932 poster on screen, slow zoom; a "2/3" stamp over a handwritten student response]

Two students answered this question. Both clearly knew their history — reparations, war guilt, crippling debt. One of them still walked out with a 2 out of 3. The point they lost is the single most common way students bleed points on the explain parts of short-answer questions. I'm going to grade three real student responses live against the rubric, using College Board's own scoring notes, so you can see exactly where the line is.

[VISUAL: the three sub-questions appearing on screen, one at a time]

Here's the setup. You're shown a 1932 election poster for the German National Socialist Party — the Nazis — picturing a distressed family, with messaging built to win votes. Part (a): identify one likely political purpose of the image. Part (b): explain one way the image illustrates the economic situation of the period after the First World War. Part (c): explain one way the rise of the Nazi party led to the Second World War. Three parts, three points — and College Board states it plainly in the scoring notes: each point is earned independently. A great part (a) never rescues a weak part (c).

[VISUAL: (a) / (b) / (c) labels with skill tags: "source purpose" / "historical context" / "causation"]

Now, what each part is really asking. Part (a) says "identify." That's the lowest bar on the test — just name the political purpose. Votes for the Nazis, anger at the current government, anything defensible. The skill is source analysis: who made this, and what do they want.

Part (b) says "explain" — a different verb, which means a different job. Describing what you see in the poster earns nothing. You have to connect the image to the bigger economic picture you already know: reparations, the Versailles settlement, unemployment, the Depression. The skill is putting the source inside its historical moment.

Part (c) also says "explain," but here the question is doing causation: the rise of one thing led to another. So you owe a mechanism — the chain of events that got from the Nazis' rise to a world war. Militarism, rearmament, invasions, expansion. Feelings are not mechanisms. The skill is causation.

[VISUAL: sample 2A response on screen; green checkmarks appearing after each part]

Sample one. Full marks — 3 out of 3. Watch what this student does.

Part (a): they write the poster was meant "to instill a dislike among the citizens for the current government" and promote Hitler as the one who would save them from economic despair. Purpose identified. Point.

Part (b): they write that "the reparations that Germany was forced to pay was the leading cause for the economic depression in Germany after the First World War." See the move? The poster shows suffering families; the student steps past the poster and names the cause behind the suffering. That's explaining. Point.

Part (c): "the socialist party increased their military and eventually invaded Poland and went to war with the allies." Military buildup, then invasion, then war — a concrete chain, not a mood. Point. Three for three. Notice the pattern: in both explain parts, the answer leaves the source and brings in real history.

[VISUAL: sample 2B response; check, check, then a red X on part (c)]

Sample two — and this one stings. 2 out of 3.

Part (a): "gaining a vote for Hitler." Point. Straightforward.

Part (b): "Germany was blamed for everything and in crippling debt." Point. They went beyond the image — war guilt, debt — so the context point lands.

Part (c): "they were so angry for taking all of the blame for the First World War." And College Board says no. Their reasoning: that line names a piece of the Nazi platform — a grievance — but it never explains how the rise of the party led to the Second World War. Anger is a motivation. The question asked for a mechanism. No rearmament, no invasions, no expansion — so the causation point dies. Two out of three.

This student knew the history. They just answered the wrong verb: they explained why the Nazis were upset when the question demanded how the Nazis caused a war.

[VISUAL: sample 2C response; check on (a), red X on (b), red X on (c)]

Sample three. 1 out of 3 — and both misses teach you something.

Part (a): the purpose was getting people to "vote for Adolf Hitler." Point.

Part (b): they write that the poster shows a distraught family to stir sympathy, and that "adults were jobless." College Board's verdict: that's just restating what's already in the source. The poster shows jobless adults; writing "the poster shows jobless adults" adds nothing. To earn the point you must go beyond the source — say something the image itself doesn't tell you, like reparations or Depression-era collapse. Describing the image is not explaining the economy. No point.

Part (c): the Nazis' rise "led to the Second World War by creating inequalities." Six words, zero mechanism. How do inequalities start a world war? There's no invasion, no military buildup, no expansion — nothing connecting the Nazis' rise to the war breaking out. No point. One for three.

[VISUAL: split screen — "describes the source" vs "explains with context"; "names a feeling" vs "names a mechanism"]

Here's the pattern, and it's the whole video. Every lost point in this set failed the same test: the student stopped one step short of the verb. In part (b), they described the source instead of connecting it to the history behind it. In part (c), they named a feeling — anger, inequality — instead of naming the mechanism. This is how students typically bleed points on this question type: abstractions instead of mechanisms. "Blame," "anger," "inequalities" — those are clouds. College Board pays for concrete chains: reparations created debt, debt deepened the depression; rearmament led to the invasion of Poland, which triggered the war. If your sentence ends at the source, or ends at a feeling, you left the point on the table.

[VISUAL: the rule as a full-screen text card]

So here's the one rule to carry into every short-answer explain part: build a because-chain that leaves the source. Source detail, then the broader cause, then the exact outcome the question asks about. And a quick self-test before you move on: could someone who only saw the image, and knows no history, have written your sentence? If yes — it's worth zero. The point is always the step beyond.

[VISUAL: old format vs new format graphic — "2024: answer 3 of 4 SAQs" → "2027: all 3 required — Q1 secondary text, Q2 primary text, Q3 map/chart/image"]

One last thing — your exam, May 2027. This question is from 2024, under the old format, and two things changed. First: no more choosing which short-answer questions to do. All three are required. Second: the source types are now locked in. Q1 uses a secondary text source, Q2 uses a primary text source, and Q3 uses a non-text source — a map, a chart, or an image. This question's stimulus is a poster — an image — so on your exam, a question like this one shows up as Q3, not Q2. But the grading logic hasn't moved an inch: identify the purpose, explain with context you bring in yourself, and for causation, give the mechanism. Same skills. New slot.
