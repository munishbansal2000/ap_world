# We Graded a Real AP World LEQ: The One Word That Cost a Student 3 Points

**Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes
**Format:** "We graded this" — live on-camera grading of official released student essays against the 6-point LEQ rubric.
**Attribution:** Question from the 2024 AP World History exam, released by College Board. Grading follows College Board's published scoring guidelines; this video is not affiliated with or endorsed by College Board.

**NARRATION:**

Two students. Same exam, same question, same revolutions — American, French, the whole greatest-hits list. One scored a perfect six out of six. The other got three.

Same facts. Different scores. The difference isn't that one student knew more history. It's that one of them answered the question that was actually asked.

Today we grade both the way a College Board reader would. One word in this prompt separates the sixes from the threes.

---

[VISUAL: The prompt appears on screen, exactly as written on the exam. The word "extent" gets highlighted.]

**NARRATION:**

Here's the prompt, from the 2024 AP World History exam: "In the period circa 1750 to 1900, discontent with monarchist and imperial rule spread around many parts of the world and led to significant political changes. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which discontent with monarchist or imperial rule was the main source of new political ideologies or systems of government during this period."

The loaded word is "extent." The prompt is not asking you to list revolutions. It's not asking whether discontent caused change — it tells you it did. It's asking: how much? Was discontent the *main* source, or just *a* source? Your thesis must take a position on degree.

That's the test: argument, causation, and the rubric's hardest point — complexity, qualifying your own argument instead of chanting one idea for forty minutes.

---

[VISUAL: The 6-point rubric as a scoreboard: Thesis (1), Contextualization (1), Evidence (2), Analysis & Reasoning (2). Label: "ESSAY 1 — SCORE: 3/6."]

**NARRATION:**

Essay one. It opens by restating the prompt almost word for word, then gives three examples: the American colonists angry over taxation without representation, the French Revolution and the Declaration of the Rights of Man, and then — Gandhi. Quote: "led non-violent peaceful protests that forced the British to make new policies and change their ideals."

Nice paragraph. One problem: Gandhi's movement is 1919 to 1948. The prompt ends at 1900. That evidence is outside the time period — a paragraph of nothing. Before you commit to an example, check the clock.

Thesis: zero. "Discontent was the main source" is the prompt with the serial numbers filed off. A thesis must name the specific new ideologies and take a position on extent. This names neither.

Contextualization: zero. Straight into examples — nothing on the Enlightenment, absolutism, or divine right. No context, no point.

Evidence: two points, and analysis gets one for cause and effect. But complexity is gone — the essay never asks whether discontent was really the *main* source. It just says yes three times.

Total: three out of six. The knowledge was there. The rubric wasn't.

---

[VISUAL: Scoreboard wipes clean. New label: "ESSAY 2 — SCORE: 4/6."]

**NARRATION:**

Essay two. Same revolutions — plus a genuinely interesting move: a counterargument. The writer suggests Enlightenment theories, not discontent, were the real source, citing Locke's influence on the Constitution. Ambitious.

Thesis: zero again. The opening says mistreatment led to discontent, which led to rebellions, and "the successful rebellions often implemented a new form of government." Which one? The thesis never names a single new ideology or system. College Board is explicit: vague nouns are not an argument.

Contextualization: one point — colonial exploitation as real background. Evidence: two points. Analysis: one for causation.

But complexity? Missed — and this is the painful part, because the counterargument was *right there*. The writer raised the Enlightenment, then swatted it: "not correct," because without discontent there'd be no need for reform. That's not engaging the other side. Complexity means genuinely weighing it — maybe discontent was the spark but Enlightenment ideas were the blueprint.

Total: four out of six. A stronger writer — but the thesis is still a restatement in a trench coat, and complexity demands you argue with yourself.

---

[VISUAL: Scoreboard wipes clean. New label: "ESSAY 3 — SCORE: 6/6."]

**NARRATION:**

Essay three. Perfect score. A six isn't about beautiful writing — it's about structure.

The thesis lands in the final paragraph: discontent with absolute monarchies and imperial systems fostered new political systems "to a great extent" through democracy, democratic ideals, and nationalism. See it? It names the actual new things and takes a position on extent. One point.

Contextualization: one point, setting up pre-revolutionary France and the thirteen colonies. Not fancy. Just there.

Evidence: two points. American Revolution, taxation without representation, the French Revolution, the Sepoy Rebellion, anti-colonial revolts in Africa. Specific, accurate, in-period, all used to support claims.

Analysis and reasoning: two points. Causation structures the whole essay. And complexity? The writer qualifies the argument across regions — discontent working differently in developed nations versus colonies, producing both democracy *and* nationalism. It's weighing the argument, not repeating it. That's the second analysis point — the one almost nobody earns.

Six out of six. The secret isn't more facts. Every paragraph is doing a rubric job.

[VISUAL: All three scoreboards side by side: 3/6, 4/6, 6/6. Tally: "Thesis restated, not argued — 2 essays. Evidence outside the time period — 1 essay. No engagement with 'extent' — all 3."]

**NARRATION:**

Three essays, same three bleeding points.

First: the restated thesis. Two of our three writers agreed with the prompt and called it an argument. Restating the question earns zero — if your thesis could have been written before you read the prompt, it's not a thesis.

Second: evidence outside the time period. The Gandhi paragraph was good history answering the wrong question. Everything you write has to live inside the prompt's dates.

Third — the big one: nobody argued with themselves. All three had a shot at the complexity point, and essay two literally wrote a counterargument. But complexity isn't mentioning the other side. It's weighing it. If your essay never says "mostly, but..." or "yes, and also...," you're leaving the hardest point behind.

---

[VISUAL: A single rule card fills the screen: "NAME IT. WEIGH IT. ARGUE WITH YOURSELF."]

**NARRATION:**

One rule for the LEQ:

Name it, weigh it, argue with yourself.

*Name it* — your thesis must name the specific new ideology or system. Not "a new form of government." Democracy. Nationalism. The reader can't give you a point for a noun you never wrote.

*Weigh it* — the prompt says "extent," so take a position on degree. That one move unlocks the thesis point and sets up everything else.

*Argue with yourself* — engage the strongest case against your position. If discontent was the main source, what else could it have been? Enlightenment ideas? Industrialization? Make the other side's best case and still hold your ground. That's the complexity point — the difference between a four and a six.

**NARRATION:**

One format note: this is a 2024 question, and back then students chose one of three LEQ prompts. Didn't like a period? You dodged it. On your exam, May 2027, there is no choice — one broad prompt, any period, any theme. You can't dodge.

But the rubric hasn't changed — same six points, same game. So the rule still holds: name it, weigh it, argue with yourself. The students who lose points in 2027 will lose them in exactly the places you just watched.
