# "We Graded This": The LEQ Where the Wrong Facts Scored a 6

**Source:** Question from the 2023 AP World History exam, released by College Board.
**Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes (~1,000 words of narration)

---

[VISUAL: Title card — "WE GRADED THIS" stamp over a handwritten LEQ essay]

**Cold open:**

Here's the most uncomfortable thing you'll learn about the LEQ today. College Board gave a perfect 6 out of 6 to an essay that says the Byzantine Empire fell around 1200, that Suleyman the Magnificent built a "Mosque of Selin II," and that Suleyman himself was raised as a Janissary. Meanwhile, a student who correctly described the Janissary system, religious tolerance, and tax farming walked away with a 4. And a third student, who also had the facts, got a 2.

[VISUAL: Scoreboard graphic — 6/6, 4/6, 2/6 with each essay's opening lines]

So the LEQ is not a fact quiz. It's an argument test. Let's grade three real 2023 answers, live, and see exactly where every point was won or lost.

[VISUAL: Prompt text on screen]

**The question:**

"In the period circa 1450 to 1750, rulers of land-based empires, such as the Mughal, the Ottoman, and the Safavid empires, used a variety of religious, political, and economic methods to legitimize and consolidate their power. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which a land-based empire successfully consolidated or expanded its power during this period."

**What it's really asking:**

The last sentence is the whole job. "Evaluate the EXTENT to which a land-based empire successfully consolidated or expanded its power." You're not being asked to list methods. You're being asked to take a position: how successful were they, and where's your evidence? That's the continuity-and-change reasoning skill. The rubric has six points: one for a thesis that actually argues something, one for historical context, two for evidence, and two for analysis — one for using reasoning to structure your argument, one for showing a complex understanding.

[VISUAL: 6-box rubric diagram — Thesis, Contextualization, Evidence ×2, Analysis ×2]

**Essay A — the one that scored 6 out of 6.**

This student picked the Ottomans and opened with a claim: "The Ottoman Empire was extremely successful in consolidating its power by legitimizing the ruler's [authority], strengthening the government, and by increasing its military in both size and strength."

[VISUAL: Essay A thesis paragraph with the sentence highlighted]

Why does it earn the thesis point? It takes a position — "extremely successful" — and lays out the argument's structure: legitimacy, government, military.

Then the very first sentence of the essay: "After the downfall of the Byzantine empire around 1200, the Ottoman Empire rose to power." The date is wrong — the city fell in 1453. But contextualization doesn't demand perfect facts; it asks you to connect your topic to a bigger picture. Rising out of the Byzantine collapse is exactly that. One sentence, one point.

Evidence: mosques and monumental architecture, the Janissary corps, the bureaucracy, Suleyman's territorial expansion — multiple specific examples, each tied to a part of the argument. Two points.

And analysis: every body paragraph runs a cause-and-effect chain — religion unified people behind the ruler, so loyalty; Janissaries loyal to the sultan, so no revolts; military expansion, so more tax revenue, so a stronger state. That's historical reasoning structuring the argument — analysis point one. The complexity point came from the layering: multiple causes and effects across three parts of the empire's power. 6 out of 6.

[VISUAL: Essay A with checkmarks filling all six rubric boxes]

Now, remember: some details here are just wrong. College Board's own notes say small errors don't sink you as long as the history building your argument is sound. Facts are the bricks. The argument is the building.

**Essay B — 4 out of 6. The "good facts, incomplete structure" essay.**

The thesis: "The Ottomans legitimized their power in 1450 to 1750 through the use of janisaries, religious tolerance and tax farming." That's a real thesis — a position plus three categories of argument. Point earned.

Evidence: Janissaries as elite soldiers trained from youth, religious tolerance explained as a stability strategy, and tax farming as auctioned tax-collection rights. Two points. Solid.

So where did it lose two points? First, contextualization. The opening says the Ottomans were "a muslim country in the middle east" and "the second longest empire in the world behind the roman empire." That describes the empire — it doesn't connect it to anything broader. No gunpowder technology, no trade routes, no Mongol successor states. One sentence situating the Ottomans in a wider process, and that's the point.

Second, complexity. The essay explains causes and effects — Janissaries won land, tolerance brought stability, tax farming brought money — so it earns the first analysis point. But it never goes deeper: no comparison to anyone, no counterexample, no connection across time periods. Three separate explanations that never talk to each other. Complexity needs depth — multiple perspectives on the same question. Without that, the sixth point stays out of reach.

[VISUAL: Essay B with four green checks, two red X's on Contextualization and Complexity]

**Essay C — 2 out of 6. The painful one.**

This student knew things — religious imagery of leaders, devshirme-style child recruitment, different tax rates for different groups. That earned both evidence points. Then it collapsed.

The thesis: "The Mughal, the Ottoman, and the Safavid empires used multiple techniques to legitimize and consolidate their power. These include the use of religious, political, and economical [methods]."

[VISUAL: Essay C thesis paragraph with annotation — "This is the prompt, repeated."]

That's the prompt, wearing a different shirt. The prompt already said rulers "used a variety of religious, political, and economic methods to legitimize and consolidate their power." Restating it is not a thesis. No position on HOW successful anyone was, no line of reasoning — zero points. And with no argument to structure, there's no historical reasoning either, so both analysis points are gone. Two points total, for facts that were actually fine.

**The miss pattern:**

Here's what this question exposes about where students bleed points on the LEQ. It's not usually the evidence — students can name the Janissaries and the Taj Mahal. It's the two moves that feel optional but aren't. First, students answer the first sentence of the prompt instead of the last one. The prompt gives the setup — "rulers used religious, political, and economic methods" — and students write about methods. But the question is the evaluation: to what extent did it work? No position on success, no argument, and the rubric punishes everything downstream.

Second, contextualization — a point students treat as fluff. It's one point out of six, and Essay A earned it with a single sentence. Connect your empire to something bigger: gunpowder weapons spreading, trade routes, the fall of a neighbor. One or two real sentences. That's it.

[VISUAL: Miss-pattern card — "Miss 1: Answering the setup, not the question" / "Miss 2: Skipping contextualization"]

**The one takeaway rule:**

Your thesis must answer the prompt's hardest word — here, "extent." So use this formula: [Empire] was [how successful] because [reason one], [reason two]. If your thesis could be swapped onto any prompt about any empire without changing a word, it's a restatement. Rewrite it until it can't.

[VISUAL: Thesis formula card — "[Empire] was [how successful] because [reason 1], [reason 2]"]

**On your exam (May 2027) — the format bridge:**

One note: in 2023 you chose one of three LEQ prompts — you could dodge a period you didn't like. On your exam, that's gone: a single broad prompt. No choice, no dodge. Which makes this skill even more important: you can't pick the question that fits your favorite facts, so arguing with whatever you're given is the whole game. The six-point rubric is unchanged. Same moves, no escape hatch.

So: take a position, give it context, connect your facts to your argument, and add one layer of depth. That's the 6. Go earn it.
