# We Graded This: The 2025 LEQ That Ate Everyone's Thesis

**Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes (~1,000 words narration)
**Concept:** Live grading of three real student essays from the released 2025 LEQ — a 6, a 4, and a 1 — against the 6-point rubric, showing exactly where points were earned or lost.
**Attribution:** Question from the 2025 AP World History exam, released by College Board.

---

**[VISUAL: Cold open — scorecard graphic, "6/6 / 4/6 / 1/6" over a scan of three essay pages]**

Here's something that should bother you. A student wrote a genuinely good essay — clear evidence, real reasoning, three solid body paragraphs — and lost two full points. Not because they didn't know the history. Because their first paragraph said nothing.

That's the 2025 LEQ on the Columbian Exchange — and today we're grading three real student answers to it — a six, a four, and a one — the way the College Board readers graded them.

**[VISUAL: The prompt appears on screen, text fades in]**

Here's the prompt, quoted from the exam: *"In the period circa 1450 to 1750, new connections between world regions led to the movement or transfer of people, animals, plants, and pathogens. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which such movements or transfers led to demographic, cultural, or social changes across the Atlantic region during this period."*

**[VISUAL: Prompt breaks into three labeled chunks — "the task," "the content," "the boundary"]**

Three things to notice. First, the task word: *evaluate the extent*. Take a position on how much these transfers actually changed things. Second, the content: Columbian Exchange stuff moving across the Atlantic — people, animals, plants, diseases. Third, the part students forget: the changes have to be *demographic, cultural, or social*. Not economic. If your essay becomes a story about silver and profits, you've drifted off the prompt.

The rubric is six points: thesis, contextualization, evidence (two points), and analysis (two points). Every point is earned independently. Keep that in mind, because it cuts both ways.

**[VISUAL: Split screen — rubric row A "Thesis/Claim" next to Essay 2A, highlighted intro paragraph]**

Essay one. Scored a six out of six. Listen to the thesis: "Such movements greatly led to demographic, cultural, and social changes across the Atlantic region, with the spread of diseases like measles and smallpox wiping out enormous populations of natives, or the vast population spike Europe gained due to the immense new crops obtained from the new world."

Notice what it's doing. It doesn't just say "changes happened." It names the *mechanisms* — diseases wiping out Indigenous populations, new crops boosting Europe's population. That's a line of reasoning: transfers caused these specific effects. Thesis point, earned.

Contextualization: the essay opens with mercantilism — states chasing economic power, competing empires, new maritime technology. That's the broader world this exchange happened inside. Point.

Evidence, two points: the essay piles up specifics — disease killing millions, enslaved Africans replacing dying Indigenous laborers, American crops fueling Europe's growth, the casta system, syncretic religions. And it doesn't just list them — it uses them to support claims, like arguing the disease deaths let Europeans colonize more securely. That's evidence doing a job, not decorating the page.

Analysis: the essay traces causes to effects throughout — pathogens causing demographic collapse, new crops causing European population growth, mixed populations causing new social hierarchies. That's the first point. And the second, the complex-understanding point: it holds two regions in view at once, showing the exchange's lopsided effects — catastrophe in the Americas, gains in Europe. Six for six.

**[VISUAL: Scorecard animates to 6/6. Transition wipe to Essay 2B]**

Essay two. Four out of six. And this is the painful one, because the body paragraphs are *good*. Read the disease paragraph: millions dead because the Americas had no immunity to Afro-Eurasian diseases, and that made Indigenous peoples easier to conquer and more colonies possible. That's cause and effect, supported by specific evidence — malaria, measles, smallpox. The culture paragraph: enslaved Africans bringing foods, languages, music, plus Christianity spreading. The social paragraph: Spain's colonies producing a hierarchy where the more European ancestry you had, the higher your status.

Two evidence points, the reasoning point — all earned. Contextualization point too, from the maritime-empires setup in the intro.

**[VISUAL: Zoom in on 2B's thesis sentences, rubric Row A criteria appears alongside]**

But the thesis point? Gone. Here's the whole thesis: "The Columbus exchange led to many demographic, cultural, and social changes across the Atlantic region during this period." It's the prompt, reworded. No claim, no position on *the extent* of change, no reason. The reader's note is blunt: it only restates information from the prompt.

And here's the sting: this student knew the history and earned every other point available. They just never made an argument — the easiest point on the rubric went unearned. A four instead of a five; the difference was one sentence with a point of view.

**[VISUAL: Scorecard animates to 4/6. Transition to Essay 2C — shorter, sparser page]**

Essay three. One out of six. Thesis: "Movements and transfers across the Atlantic region led to several things including, demographic, cultural, and social changes." Same problem, worse version — a restatement with even less shape. No point. Zero for context — the essay dives straight in. Evidence: it names corn and smallpox — two specific things, so that's one point. But the second evidence point requires evidence that *supports an argument*, and there's no argument to support. The sentences around the evidence are things like "the demographic changed because of people reproducing." That's not reasoning; that's a shrug. Zero analysis points. Total: one.

**[VISUAL: All three scorecards side by side — 6, 4, 1. Thesis row highlighted red on the 4 and the 1]**

Here's the miss pattern: the losing essays restate the prompt and call it a thesis. The prompt asks about "demographic, cultural, or social changes," and the student writes back "demographic, cultural, and social changes" — and thinks the job is done. Two out of three essays here lost the thesis point that way, including a four-point essay that was otherwise excellent. It keeps happening because a restated prompt *feels* like an argument under time pressure — it uses the prompt's words, it's true, it sits in the intro. But the rubric doesn't ask "is this true." It asks for a claim with a because.

And the second bleed, visible in essay three: facts without a job. Naming corn and smallpox earns you one evidence point. But evidence only pays full value when it's *doing argumentative work* — proving something about the extent of change. Every fact needs a sentence after it saying what it proves. No orphan facts.

**[VISUAL: The one rule appears as on-screen text, narrator's voice over it]**

So the one takeaway rule: **restating is not arguing.** Before you write body paragraph one, read your thesis and ask: "Could someone disagree with this?" If it just repeats the question back, add the *because*. That's the whole fix — and on this question, it was worth the difference between a four and a five, and between a one and a three.

**[VISUAL: "May 2027" graphic — single LEQ prompt card, "no choice" stamp]**

One last thing: this question is from 2025, and your exam is May 2027. Back then, students picked one of three LEQ prompts. On your exam, it's a single broad prompt — no choice, no dodging. The rubric is unchanged: same six points. You don't get to pick the prompt that fits your skills anymore — you have to build a real argument on whatever shows up. Practice the because.
