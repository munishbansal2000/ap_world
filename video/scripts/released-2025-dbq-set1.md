# A 4/7 and a 2/7, Graded Live: The Africa Technology DBQ (2025)

**Target runtime:** ~7 minutes (~1,000 words of narration)

**Attribution:** Question from the 2025 AP World History exam, released by College Board. Not affiliated with or endorsed by College Board.

## COLD OPEN

[VISUAL: black screen. Two big numbers fade in: "4/7" and "2/7"]

Two real students. Same seven documents. Same question.

The first one scored 4 out of 7 — and did the hardest move on the rubric twice, and got zero points for it.

The second one scored 2, and built part of their argument on a photograph they read backwards.

Today we're grading both live against the actual 7-point rubric, with College Board's own notes as the referee — so you can see exactly where points go to die.

## THE QUESTION

[VISUAL: prompt text on screen, single clean line]

The 2025 prompt was one sentence: "Evaluate the extent to which new transportation and/or communication technologies affected African societies during the period circa 1850 to 1960."

## WHAT IT'S REALLY ASKING

[VISUAL: the word "extent" highlighted; scale graphic]

That word means you can't just list effects — you need a position on HOW MUCH and IN WHAT WAYS. Your thesis has to take a stance.

This question tests the full toolkit: a defensible claim, documents supporting an argument rather than summarized, and showing you understand where each document comes from.

[VISUAL: seven document icons, one-line paraphrase under each]

The packet, in plain words: Temne leaders in Sierra Leone write in 1896 that a British hut tax — funding roads and a rail line — is more than their people can pay; an 1899 photograph shows two South African diamond miners, Western-dressed and just paid in British coins; 1900 telegrams: Ashanti fighters cut telegraph wires and blockade Kumasi; British commanders rush troops from Lagos and Sierra Leone to restore communication; a 1908 steamship-company brochure sells tourists a rail ride from Alexandria to Cairo, past farms, cotton mills, and a modernized city; a 1914 British report finds tuberculosis worst along the railway lines, where mining crowded workers into packed towns; a 1915 book by British officials: Gold Coast railways exploded trade and cocoa, opened European firms in Kumasi, and tied the colony to Britain by cable; and a Nigerian historian's 2004 memoir of 1950s–60s Ibadan: trains carried cocoa to London and New York and brought in settlers from across Nigeria, Lebanon, Syria, and India.

## ESSAY 1 — THE 4/7

[VISUAL: Essay 1 on screen. Score counter starts at 0/7]

This first student knew what they were doing — mostly. Let's score it.

Thesis — point. The intro argues railways were mostly positive, especially in trade, but resistance and disease worked against them. One for one.

Contextualization — point: pre-1850 Africa relatively isolated, then European industrialization and the scramble for resources bringing new technology. Two for two.

Evidence from documents — both points: six documents described, five used to support real arguments — the hut tax burden, Cairo's economic boost, Ibadan's growth, Gold Coast trade, railways spreading disease. Three for three, then four.

Outside evidence — missed, and it hurts, because they tried. Quote: "There were many other resistance movements to British imperialism in Africa and it was not only the Ashanti." A gesture, not evidence — no names, no places, no specifics. Vague does not earn. Still four.

Sourcing — missed, and here's the painful part: the student attempts it TWICE. On the Cairo brochure, they write it shows "the westernization of the City of Cairo" — but that repeats the source line, and quoting the brochure's own sales language is not analysis. On the tuberculosis report, its purpose was to "accurately detail the cause" of the disease — but College Board's note: all they added was the word "accurately."

Sourcing isn't describing the document — it's explaining why the author's viewpoint or purpose matters FOR YOUR ARGUMENT. They knew the move; they described instead of explaining. Still four.

Complexity — missed. They cover positive AND negative effects, but College Board says it plainly: both sides without nuance isn't complex understanding. No weighing, no qualifying, no tension resolved. Four out of seven stands.

[VISUAL: scoreboard locks at 4/7. Outside evidence, sourcing, complexity flash red]

Total: 4 out of 7 — three points away on exactly the moves most students miss.

## ESSAY 2 — THE 2/7

[VISUAL: Essay 2 on screen. Score counter starts at 0/7]

The second essay opens with a misread that poisons everything after it.

Thesis — point, barely: new technologies eased travel to jobs, eased trade, and drove urbanization. Three categories — enough of a position.

Contextualization — missed. No broader background at all.

Evidence — half earned. Three documents described gets the first point, but the second needs four documents used to SUPPORT AN ARGUMENT — this writer only manages two.

On the miners' photograph, they write that the men had to walk back from their jobs — "100 to 400 miles" home — and argue new transport made commuting easier and boosted earnings. But the document shows men standing on a road after being paid; the caption describes their journey home. The student read the photograph backwards, so the argument collapses. And even if it hadn't, three documents in an argument is not four. Point missed.

Outside evidence — missed. Nothing beyond the documents. Sourcing — missed; no attempt at all. Complexity — missed; no attempt.

[VISUAL: scoreboard locks at 2/7]

Total: 2 out of 7.

## THE MISS PATTERN

[VISUAL: Chief Reader stats on screen: sourcing 0.39/1, outside evidence 0.47/1, complexity 0.15/1]

The 2025 Chief Reader report says the three worst DBQ components nationally are sourcing — earned about 39 percent of the time — outside evidence at 47 percent, and complexity at a brutal 15 percent. Both essays bled exactly there — and show you HOW.

Essay 1 is the sourcing failure in its purest form: the student attempted it twice and scored zero, because they described instead of explaining. Their outside-evidence miss is the same disease one level down: "many other resistance movements" names nothing, so it proves nothing. And their complexity miss is the most misunderstood lesson on the rubric: covering positives and negatives is not nuance — nuance is weighing, qualifying, resolving tension.

Essay 2 shows the evidence trap from the other side: describing earns the first point, but the second demands four documents each doing work inside an argument — and one document read backwards killed a whole argument.

## THE ONE TAKEAWAY

[VISUAL: on-screen rule card: "Explain, don't describe."]

One rule, and it covers all three misses: explain, don't describe.

Use the document — then say what it PROVES for your claim, not what it says.

Source the document — then say why the author's viewpoint or purpose changes how you read it, and what that means for your argument.

Bring outside knowledge — then name the specific thing. A name, a place, an event.

And when you see two forces pulling different ways, weigh them — qualify, compare, resolve.

Explain, don't describe. It's the difference between a 4 and a 7.
