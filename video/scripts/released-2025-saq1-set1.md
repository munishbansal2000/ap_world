## [COLD OPEN — 0:00]

[VISUAL: score stamps appearing — "3/3", "2/3", "1/3" — over the question header.]

What if I told you that on this question, one student got a fact straight-up wrong and still walked away with all three points, while another student, who clearly knew their stuff, earned just one?

This really happened, and we know why — College Board released the official grading notes. We're grading three real student responses together, live against the rubric, and by the end you'll see the real lesson: the rubric isn't rewarding how much you know. It's rewarding whether you gave the question exactly what it asked for.

## [THE QUESTION — 0:45]

[VISUAL: the question prompt on screen; paraphrased passage summary appears beside it.]

Here's the setup. A secondary source argues that the discovery of the Americas sent shockwaves across the Atlantic — wrecking African trade networks, reshaping the American economy, and crushing Indigenous peoples.

Part (a) asks you to identify one claim the author makes in the first paragraph about what the discovery of the Americas did to Africa.

Part (b) asks you to describe one economic change in the Americas tied to the developments discussed in the second paragraph.

Part (c) asks you to explain one reason why, in the author's words, American Indians became "victims of the discovery of America."

Three parts, three different jobs. Let's see what each one is really asking.

## [WHAT EACH PART IS REALLY ASKING — 1:30]

[VISUAL: three labels animate in — "IDENTIFY", "DESCRIBE", "EXPLAIN" — with the scope under each: Africa / the Americas / the reason why.]

Part (a) says *identify*. That means: pull a claim the author makes. Your opinion doesn't count here, and your outside knowledge barely matters. Report what the passage says happened to Africa — the collapse of its gold markets, the decline of its trading cities and empires, the abandonment of old trade routes.

Part (b) says *describe*. That means giving the characteristics of one specific economic change, and in one specific place: the Americas. Think the spread of the plantation system, or the growing demand for enslaved African labor as Indigenous labor collapsed.

Part (c) says *explain*. How or why. That needs a mechanism — a "because." Disease arriving from Afro-Eurasia, forced labor systems like the encomienda, land seized for European profit, forced conversion. Pick one and connect it.

## [LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 1A: 3/3 — 2:30]

[VISUAL: Sample 1A's response on screen; part-by-part checkmarks appear as each is graded.]

Grading time. Response one. For (a), the student writes:

> "Africa suffered because Europeans no longer needed the gold that they traded."

Grade: point. But wait — the passage actually says it was the discovery of *silver* in the Americas that destroyed Africa's gold markets, not gold. The student got the metal wrong, and it didn't matter. College Board's own commentary says the identification was accurate enough. So on an *identify* question, you're judged on whether you caught the author's claim — not on flawless recall of every detail.

For (b), the student describes the shift toward cash-crop production on plantations controlled by Europeans, worked by enslaved labor. Point — that's an economic change in the Americas, described.

For (c), they explain that Indigenous people were defeated and forced into labor under systems like the encomienda. Point — there's a mechanism, stated plainly.

Three for three — and remember, this student got a fact wrong and still aced it.

## [LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 1B: 2/3 — 3:50]

[VISUAL: Sample 1B on screen; the part (b) section gets an X.]

Response two. For (a), the student identifies the collapse of Africa's gold market, the abandonment of cities like Timbuktu, and the weakening of the Songhai Empire. Point — that's solid work.

But (b)... here's the answer:

> "the Americas became the main supplier of food, goods, and metals to european empires."

No point. And College Board's commentary is blunt: that statement is too sweeping and historically incorrect. The student went for big-picture impressiveness instead of one specific, defensible change. "Main supplier of food, goods, and metals" sounds like an answer, but it doesn't actually describe anything. On a *describe* question, vague and grandiose is worse than small and precise.

For (c), they point to the encomienda system putting Indigenous people to work. Point — the commentary calls it oversimplified, but accurate enough.

Two out of three. One point, lost to sweeping.

## [LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 1C: 1/3 — 5:10]

[VISUAL: Sample 1C on screen; parts (a) and (b) get X marks.]

Now the painful one. For (a), the student writes:

> "America had all the silver and gold Europe needed."

Is that true? Basically, yes. But it earns zero points. Why? Because the question asked for a claim about the *effect on Africa*. The student gave the cause — what Europe got — without the effect: that this destroyed Africa's gold markets and trade networks. They answered the premise, not the question. One missing half-sentence. One lost point.

For (b), the student writes about Mediterranean trade in cloth, beads, leather, and metals being replaced by the slave trade. Again — zero. And notice the miss: the content isn't really wrong. But the question asked for a change in the *Americas*, and the student answered with a change in the Mediterranean. Wrong ocean, zero points.

Then (c): one bare sentence — "Europeans took their land." And that earns the point. Barely. The commentary calls it oversimplified and poorly articulated, but minimally sufficient.

So this student clearly knew things — and lost two points because both answers landed outside the question's scope.

## [THE MISS PATTERN — 6:15]

[VISUAL: big text — "Points weren't lost to ignorance. They were lost to scope." Arrows show the mismatches: "Africa, not Europe" / "the Americas, not the Mediterranean" / "specific, not sweeping."]

What's the pattern? Points weren't lost to ignorance. They were lost to scope mismatch — answering a slightly different question than the one on the page.

In (a), the scope is Africa — not Europe's new wealth. Students give the premise instead of the claim.

In (b), the scope is the Americas — not Europe, not the Mediterranean. And the verb is *describe* — not sweep. "Main supplier of everything" is a slogan, not a description.

In (c), the bar is low: one clear mechanism earns it. Overwriting wastes time; one real "because" gets the point.

The bleed isn't knowledge. It's alignment: verb plus place.

## [THE TAKEAWAY RULE — 6:55]

[VISUAL: the rule on screen.]

So your one rule for every SAQ: **read the question twice.**

First read: find the verb — identify, describe, explain. That's your move.

Second read: find the scope — where, and to whom. That's your target.

A claim from the passage, not your opinion. A change in the place it asked about. A reason with a mechanism. Give the question exactly what it asked for, and the rubric has nowhere to take points from you.
