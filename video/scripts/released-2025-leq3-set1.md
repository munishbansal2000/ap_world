# We Graded This: 2025 LEQ 3 — Were Economic Rivalries the Real Reason Europe Expanded?

**Target runtime:** ~7 minutes (1,100 words of narration)

---

[VISUAL: Title card — "WE GRADED THIS" stamp; scoreboard showing 6 / 3 / 1]

Three real student essays. Same question. One scored a perfect six. One scored a three. One scored a one.

And here's the painful part: the student who scored the one knew a lot — the mita system, the Protestant Reformation, the Sunni-Shi'a split. Accurate history, real studying. They just never answered the question that was actually on the page. Five points, gone on technique.

Today we're grading all three live, point by point, using College Board's own scoring notes. Question from the 2025 AP World History exam, released by College Board.

[VISUAL: The prompt on screen; the phrase "evaluates the extent" highlighted]

"In the period circa 1450 to 1750, economic, political, and religious rivalries led many imperial states around the world to expand their territories and influence. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which economic rivalries were the primary motivation for the expansion of European empires during this period."

What is it really asking? Not "list reasons Europe expanded." The load-bearing phrase is "evaluate the extent" — how much? Mostly money, or mostly something else? Take a position on a scale, and show the other side too.

Skills tested: a thesis with a real line of reasoning, contextualization, two levels of evidence — naming examples, then using them — and historical reasoning like causation and comparison, building toward a complex, two-sided evaluation.

[VISUAL: Rubric sidebar appears — six boxes: Thesis / Context / Evidence / Evidence + Argument / Reasoning / Complexity]

Essay one — the six.

[VISUAL: Essay 3A fades in, thesis sentence highlighted]

The thesis lands: "While economic motives including new economic ideals as well as drive for domination over certain trade motivated the expansion of European empires, missionary values on the spread on religion also pushed for the European expansion." That's a claim with categories — economic AND religious motives. Point earned.

Context: centuries of Europe-Asia contact, where "Islamic and Chinese inventions had made their way through trade to Europe, giving them access to advanced navigation systems and ship building." Elaborated, relevant, in-period — a described development, not a passing phrase. Point earned.

Then evidence: the spice trade, Prince Henry and Vasco da Gama, the Portuguese-Dutch fight over the Indian Ocean trade, Dutch fluyt ships, mercantilism, American silver. Plenty of specific examples — point earned.

But the essay actually uses them. The Portuguese-Dutch rivalry backs the claim that competition drove expansion; mercantilism backs the global scramble for wealth; the missionaries in Japan back the religious counter-argument. Evidence tied to arguments — second point earned.

Analysis: the economic paragraph runs on causation; the religious paragraph compares the two motives. First reasoning point earned. And the whole essay weighs economic against non-economic motives — the two-sided evaluation the prompt asked for. Complexity earned. Six for six.

[VISUAL: Scoreboard — 3A: six green boxes]

Essay two — the three.

[VISUAL: Essay 3B, thesis highlighted]

Thesis: "A huge reason for the expansion of European empires was economic rivalries since it motivated them to gain more natural resources from their colonies and because they could tax these colonies." Categories: resources and taxation. Point earned.

Contextualization: the essay says Spain and others "were exploring different continents and setting up colonies in places like Africa." Too vague, per College Board — and it drags in the Scramble for Africa (late 1800s, outside the period). Point lost.

Evidence: first tier earned — British exploitation of India, Dutch joint-stock companies taxing Indonesia, British taxation of the Americas. Two-plus specific examples. Point.

Second tier: lost. The Scramble for Africa material is outside the period, so it can't support anything. And the Dutch-taxing-Indonesia example never connects to an argument about economic rivalries. That's the split between the two evidence points: point one asks "do you know things," point two asks "can you use them."

Reasoning: the essay frames an argument around causation — economic motives causing expansion — so the first analysis point lands. But no second side, no weighing. Complexity lost.

Three out of six — the most common LEQ profile: thesis, some evidence, some reasoning, but every point that requires connecting things slips away.

[VISUAL: Scoreboard — 3B: three green, three red]

Essay three — the one.

[VISUAL: Essay 3C]

The thesis barely earns: "economic rivalries were the primary motivation for the expansion of European empires due to religious conflicts, developed labor systems and new discovery of land." "Due to religious conflicts" contradicts the claim — but the graders treated it as a slip and found a line of reasoning in labor systems and new lands. Sloppy wording didn't kill this essay. What came next did. Contextualization: one vague Columbian Exchange sentence that never connects to the prompt. Point lost.

Evidence: zero — and this stings, because the writer studied. Accurate paragraphs on the Reformation, on the Sunni-Shi'a split, the Safavids, the Mughals. All real history, all worth nothing: none of it is about economic rivalries driving European expansion. The mita system counted as one piece of evidence, but there's no second piece, and nothing supports an argument about the prompt. Both reasoning points fall too. One out of six — not empty, just answering a different question.

[VISUAL: Scoreboard — 3C: one green box, five red]

[VISUAL: "WHERE THE POINTS BLEED" — three rows appear one by one]

The bleeding happens in the same three places.

First: vague or wandering context. Essay two lost it with one foggy sentence and a detour a century outside the period. Context must be a described development inside the question's timeframe.

Second: evidence without an argument. Essays two and three both knew real facts, but neither connected them to economic rivalries. Most students stop at naming things — the rubric rewards using them.

Third: answering your own question. Essay three was a real essay about religion and labor in early modern Eurasia — just not this prompt. Every paragraph must serve your argument about economic rivalries, or it's decoration.

[VISUAL: Full-screen card — THE "SO WHAT?" RULE]

The takeaway rule: every piece of evidence gets a "so what." The second you write a fact — Dutch fluyts, silver, missionaries — add one sentence explaining why it proves your point about economic rivalries. Evidence without the "so what" is just a list, and College Board does not give points for lists.

That one habit is worth up to three points — evidence point two, reasoning, and usually complexity, because connecting evidence to both sides of your argument is what complexity looks like.

[VISUAL: "ON YOUR EXAM — MAY 2027" banner]

One format note: this question is from 2025, when students chose one of three LEQ prompts — you could dodge a weak period or theme. On your exam, the LEQ is a single broad prompt. No choice. No dodging.

Which makes this question perfect practice: broad, spanning the whole early modern period, forcing an evaluation — exactly the shape of the single prompt you'll face. Same rubric: six points, same rows. Thesis still needs a line of reasoning, context still needs elaboration, evidence still has two tiers.

So drill the move that separated the six from the three and the one: take any fact you know cold, and practice attaching the "so what." That's the whole game.
