# We Graded This: 2025 SAQ3 Set 1 — Gunpowder Empires

**Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes
**Narration target:** ~1,000 words
**Attribution:** Question from the 2025 AP World History exam, released by College Board.

---

## [COLD OPEN]

[VISUAL: Scoreboard graphic — "Response 1: 3/3" "Response 2: 2/3" "Response 3: 1/3". Clean on-screen cards, no handwriting.]

Three students, one question: a 3/3, a 2/3, and a 1/3. Nobody lost a point for getting the history wrong. Every point died the same way — the student described what was happening in the empire instead of what the ruler did.

We're grading all three live, the way College Board's own scorers did, hunting the exact sentence where each point slipped away.

## [THE QUESTION]

[VISUAL: The question on screen, Parts A / B / C. Highlight the verbs as they're named.]

Briefly: all three parts are about the big Muslim empires — Ottomans, Safavids, Mughals — between roughly 1300 and 1600.

Part A: identify one technological or military factor behind their expansion.
Part B: explain one way Muslim rulers used economic policies to generate revenue.
Part C: explain one reason some Muslim rulers adopted tolerant policies toward religious or ethnic minorities.

Before we look at a single answer, underline the verb in each part. Part A says *identify*. Parts B and C say *explain*. That difference is the whole game. "Identify" means name it — one defensible thing, done. "Explain" means how or why: the mechanism or the motive.

Underline the actor too. Parts B and C both say *Muslim rulers* — the subject of the sentence you're graded on is a ruler making a decision.

## [SAMPLE 1 — THE 3/3]

[VISUAL: "Response 1". Split view: student text left, rubric checklist right. Green check appears per part.]

Response one is our control group — it shows what "enough" looks like.

Part A: the student identifies gunpowder weapons and ties them to the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople, the gateway into Europe. Point earned, per College Board's commentary. Name the factor, tie it to expansion, move on.

Part B: the student explains tax farming — private bidders winning the right to collect taxes in a district and keeping anything above the assessed amount, plus local leaders collecting for the Mughal state. The commentary credits it because the student explains *how the policy worked* — the ruler outsources collection; the revenue still flows to the state.

Part C: tolerance earned respect, kept the peace, kept power with the ruler — backed up with the millet system, where communities ran their own affairs but stayed loyal. Point earned.

Three parts, three points. Remember how little effort full credit took — the next two responses are missing exactly that much.

## [SAMPLE 2 — THE 2/3]

[VISUAL: "Response 2". Part B gets a red X. Pull up the scored excerpt.]

Response two is strong on the outside parts. Part A: gunpowder — College Board says identifying the technology alone was enough. Part C: religious diversity meant intolerance would risk "more internal conflicts." Point earned — a motive, stated plainly.

Now Part B. Here's where the point died:

[VISUAL: Excerpt on screen, quoted: "Using trading routes such as the Silk Roads, Maritime trading, or the Sand roads, allowed for Muslim merchants to trade with others in which they brought wealth back to the empire..."]

Merchants traded. Wealth came back. True — and worth zero points. College Board's ruling: the response "does not explain how Muslim rulers used merchants or what policies rulers adopted toward merchants."

The question asked what *rulers* did — what *policy* they adopted. The student answered with background economic activity. A trade route is not a policy, and "wealth came back" is not a ruler's decision. This student answered "how did Muslim empires get rich?" instead of "how did *rulers* use *economic policies*?"

The fix takes one sentence: keep the trade scene, add the ruler's move — rulers *taxed* that trade. Customs duties, caravan levies, tax farming, tribute collection. Trade is the setting. The policy is the point.

## [SAMPLE 3 — THE 1/3]

[VISUAL: "Response 3". Parts B and C get red Xs. Slow down slightly — this is the autopsy.]

Response three banks Part A — gunpowder, "a huge technological advantage" over spears and swords. Then both explanations collapsed, each in a different way.

Part B: caravans protecting cargo, tolerance toward minorities, syncretism growing markets. College Board: none of it connects to an economic policy adopted by a ruler. Notice the drift — the student is answering Part C's topic inside Part B's box. Skip underlining the verb, and you pour everything you know into whichever part feels closest. The scorer can't give you a point for the right content in the wrong part.

Part C is the most painful — the student clearly studied Akbar's tolerance toward Hindus and Sikhs. Then the *why*:

[VISUAL: Excerpt on screen, quoted: "the caste system prohibited different social classes from communicating, therefore... they couldn't come together to form any sort of resistance to the Muslims."]

College Board's verdict: at best, that explains why Hindus *couldn't unite against* Mughal rule — the opposite direction. The question asks why the *ruler chose* tolerance. Motive versus circumstance.

What would have earned it? One rubric reason: tolerance meant fewer revolts, protecting the ruler's legitimacy. Or rulers wanted minority merchants' economic contribution and the jizya taxes non-Muslims paid. Or the military contribution — Rajput warriors, Ottoman Janissaries. Pick one, state the ruler's motive, done.

## [THE MISS PATTERN]

[VISUAL: The three failed excerpts side by side — merchants, caravans, caste system. One label stamps across: "What existed" vs "What the ruler decided."]

Here's the pattern — the single most common way students lose points on explanation parts.

The question names an actor and a decision: *rulers* used *policies*; rulers *adopted* tolerance. The students respond with the scenery: trade existed, caravans ran, the caste system was there. Historically true, worth nothing — a description of the world is not an explanation of a decision.

If your answer sentence could be true in a world where the ruler did nothing — "trade happened," "merchants were rich" — it cannot earn an explanation point. The point lives in the ruler's move: taxed, farmed, appointed, tolerated *in order to*.

Part A, the "identify" part, was nearly free — all three banked it by naming gunpowder. All the bleeding happened on the "explain" parts. That's the design of the question.

## [THE ONE TAKEAWAY]

[VISUAL: On-screen rule card. "Answer the actor and the verb."]

The rule: **answer the actor and the verb.**

Before you write a word, underline who the question is about and what it asks you to do. *Muslim rulers* — every sentence needs a ruler doing something. *Explain* — every answer needs a how or a why, not just a what.

Then run the nothing-test: if your sentence stays true with the ruler deleted, rewrite it with the ruler in it. "Merchants traded on the Silk Roads" fails. "Rulers taxed Silk Road trade through customs duties" passes. Same knowledge. One earns the point, one doesn't. That's the entire difference between the 3/3 and the 1/3 — not what they knew, but whether the ruler was in the sentence.

## [2027 FORMAT BRIDGE]

[VISUAL: "On YOUR exam — May 2027" banner. Old format vs new format side by side.]

One bridge before you go: this is a 2025 question from the old format, when SAQ 3 was no-stimulus and you *chose* between two of them. You could skip the one you didn't like.

On your May 2027 exam, two things change. First: no choice — all three SAQs are required, so you can't dodge a question type. Second: Question 3 now *always* comes with a non-text source — a map, a chart, or an image. This question might sit next to a map of the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires around 1600, and at least one part would ask you to use it.

What does NOT change: the skills. Identify still means name it; explain still means how or why. The map gives you better raw material, but the actor-and-verb rule still decides who gets the point.
