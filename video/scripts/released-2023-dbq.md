## COLD OPEN

[VISUAL: black screen. Two big numbers fade in: "4/7" and "2/7"]

Two real students. Same documents. Same question.

The first one scored 4 out of 7 — and here's the painful part: they were exactly one document away from a 5.

The second one scored 2, even though they mentioned all seven documents.

Today we're grading both live against the actual 7-point rubric, with College Board's own scoring notes as the referee. If you're writing a DBQ in May 2027, these two essays show you the exact places where points go to die.

## THE QUESTION

[VISUAL: prompt text on screen, single clean line]

The 2023 prompt was one sentence: "Evaluate the extent to which foreign involvement led to the collapse of the Qing Empire."

## WHAT IT'S REALLY ASKING

[VISUAL: the word "extent" highlighted; two buckets labeled FOREIGN and INTERNAL]

That word "extent" is doing all the work. This isn't "list the ways foreigners hurt the Qing." You have to take a position: how much of the collapse was foreign involvement — and how much was something else?

The documents hand you both sides. On one hand: opium, unequal treaties, foreign powers carving up China. On the other: banditry, rural poverty, and Han anger at Manchu rulers. Your job is an argument about which mattered more. That's what this question tests: a defensible claim, documents used as support, and showing you understand where each document comes from.

[VISUAL: seven document icons, one-line paraphrase under each]

A quick tour of the packet, in plain words: local officials around 1850 begging for troops because bandits had seized a district capital; Karl Marx blaming British opium for Qing weakness and the Taiping Rebellion; a Qing foreign office memo from 1878 about foreigners' legal protections in China; village elders in 1896 reporting that poor farm workers were turning to banditry; a 1907 revolutionary call to expel the Manchu and build a republic; a 1911 cartoon showing China as a tiger being carved up by Westerners; and the 1912 abdication decree that ended the dynasty.

## ESSAY 1 — THE 4/7

[VISUAL: Essay 1 on screen. Score counter starts at 0/7]

This first student wrote a confident essay. Let's score it row by row.

Thesis — point. The intro argues that foreign influence from the Manchus up close and from Britain, France, and Japan from afar caused the collapse, with native revolts sparking on both fronts. That's a real position with real categories. One for one.

Contextualization — point. They open with Manchu-versus-Han ethnic tension as the backdrop the whole essay sits inside. That's broader background, and it earns. Two for two.

Evidence from documents — point. They accurately describe the content of documents 2, 3, and 6. Three for three.

Now watch this one. The next point needs six documents supporting an argument. Count with me: they use 2, 3, and 6 on the foreign side, and 5 and 7 on the Manchu-tension side. That's five. Five is not six. Point missed — and this one stings, because the student actually WAS arguing with the documents. They just needed one more.

Outside evidence — point. They bring in Britain's demand for Chinese tea and porcelain, the Opium Wars, and the drain on Chinese resources — specific, relevant, outside the documents. So we're at four out of seven.

Sourcing — missed. And this is the moment most students get wrong. For document 3, the treaty memo, the student explains that foreigners weren't subject to Chinese courts and writes, quote, "So basically you can commit a crime like stealing or murder in China but China won't punish you. This gave free reign to foreigners in China which upset the local Chinese peoples."

That's a sharp description of the document's historical situation. But the rubric doesn't ask you to describe the situation — it asks you to explain why the source's point of view, purpose, or situation matters TO YOUR ARGUMENT. The student described the treaty. They never said "and this matters to my claim because..." Describing is not explaining. No point.

Complexity — missed. There's no qualifying, no weighing of multiple forces against each other. The student had both pieces — foreign pressure AND Manchu-Han tension — but never connected them into a nuanced argument.

[VISUAL: scoreboard locks at 4/7. The missing rows — 6-doc argument, sourcing, complexity — flash red]

Total: 4 out of 7.

## ESSAY 2 — THE 2/7

[VISUAL: Essay 2 on screen. Score counter starts at 0/7]

The second essay is shorter, and it makes the most common DBQ mistake in existence. Let's score it.

Thesis — point, barely. The last line of the first paragraph says foreign involvement took away China's respect and made its laws seem meaningless. That's a position. One point.

Contextualization — missed. No broader background anywhere. Straight into the documents.

Evidence — half earned. They mention all seven documents. All seven! And they still only get the first evidence point, because every mention is a summary: document 4 is about rich people ignoring the poor; document 6 is a picture of China falling apart. Not once does the writer tell the reader what any of it PROVES.

Here's the rule: the second evidence point isn't "touch six documents." It's "use six documents to SUPPORT AN ARGUMENT." A list of summaries is not an argument. No point.

Outside evidence — missed. Nothing beyond the documents. Sourcing — missed. Complexity — missed. No attempt at any of them.

[VISUAL: scoreboard locks at 2/7]

Total: 2 out of 7.

## THE MISS PATTERN

[VISUAL: Chief Reader stats on screen: sourcing 0.39/1, complexity 0.15/1, evidence-beyond-docs 0.47/1]

Here's why these two essays matter beyond themselves. The 2025 Chief Reader report says the three worst DBQ components nationally are sourcing — students earn it roughly 39 percent of the time — outside evidence at 47 percent, and complexity at just 15 percent. Both essays bled exactly there.

Essay 1 shows you the sourcing failure in its purest form: describe the document all you want, but if you never connect the source back to your argument, it's zero.

Essay 2 shows you the evidence failure: seven documents touched, zero arguments made. A reader can't give you points for an argument you never made.

## THE ONE TAKEAWAY

[VISUAL: on-screen rule card: "Use the document. Then use the SOURCE."]

One rule. For every document you use, do two things.

First: say what it shows and what that PROVES in your argument — not a summary, a claim backed by the document. That earns the evidence points.

Second: say why the source itself matters — who wrote it, why they wrote it, what was happening around them — and how that fact about the source strengthens or complicates your claim. That earns sourcing.

And if you find yourself weighing two forces against each other — foreign pressure versus internal rot — write that tension down. That's complexity, and it's the rarest point on the exam.
