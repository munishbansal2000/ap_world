# We Graded This: The 2025 Tea SAQ That Exposed Everyone's Favorite Mistake

## COLD OPEN

Three students. Same question about tea. One scores a perfect 3 out of 3. One scores 2. One scores 1.

Here's the painful part: the student who scored 1 out of 3 wrote the *longest* answer. And the student who lost a point on an otherwise perfect response didn't lose it because they didn't know enough history — they lost it because they knew the *wrong century's* history.

This question looks like a freebie. A passage, three short tasks, one point each. But it has a kill zone, and College Board's own graders just showed us exactly where it is. Let's grade these live.

## THE QUESTION

The setup is a secondary source — a historian's passage — arguing that between the late 1800s and early 1900s, India displaced China as Britain's main supplier of tea. Production costs shifted, colonial plantations expanded, Chinese tea farmers were devastated, and, in the author's words, China "descended into a chaotic period."

Then three tasks, one point each:

A. Identify one argument the author makes about tea production.
B. Describe one likely reason for the change in Britain's tea imports — *as outlined by the author*.
C. Explain how one piece of evidence *not in the passage* would support the author's claim that China "descended into a chaotic period."

[VISUAL: The three sub-prompts appear on screen, one at a time, with A / B / C labels.]

## WHAT EACH PART IS REALLY ASKING

Part A is a reading check. The skill is identifying a historian's argument. The answer lives *inside the passage* — India dethroned China as Britain's tea supplier, Indian production costs fell below China's, Chinese farmers were devastated. You don't need outside knowledge. You need to read.

Part B is one level deeper, and there's a phrase most students skim right past: "as outlined by the author." That phrase is a leash. It means your reason has to come from the passage — colonization opening up plantations, lower production costs in India, the shorter shipping distance — not from your own head. Invent your own reason and you get zero, even if it sounds plausible.

Part C flips the whole game. Now you *leave* the passage. Your outside evidence has to pass three checks: it can't be in the passage, it has to be inside the time window the question names — the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — and it has to be specific enough to actually support the claim. The Opium Wars and the unequal treaties? Great. The 1911 Revolution and the warlord era? Great. The Boxer Rebellion? Great.

[VISUAL: Diagram — Parts A and B point INTO the passage; Part C points OUT of it, with three checkmarks: "not in passage," "in the time window," "specific."]

## LIVE GRADING: SAMPLE ONE — 3 OUT OF 3

[VISUAL: Response appears on screen with a running score counter.]

Part A: the student writes that India became Britain's largest tea supplier, and backs it with the passage's own numbers — Chinese exports collapsing while Indian exports surged. That's the author's argument, pulled straight from the text. Point.

Part B: lower production costs in India than in China, with the passage's detail that costs were roughly equal in 1872 before Chinese costs rose. That's the author's reason, described, not just named. Point.

Part C: the student brings the Opium Wars, the unequal treaties, and foreign spheres of influence, then connects them to disorder and the Boxer Rebellion. Outside the passage, inside the time window, specific, and it supports the chaos claim. Point.

Three for three. Notice what's *not* here: no fancy vocabulary, no extra knowledge showing off. This student just did exactly what each part asked. Boring? Maybe. Perfect? Yes.

## LIVE GRADING: SAMPLE TWO — 2 OUT OF 3

Part A: the student says India's cheaper tea became Britain's main supply while China's industry crumbled. A little loose, but it identifies the author's argument. Point — College Board's commentary confirms it.

Part B: Britain wanted the cheaper product and got more tea for less money. That's the author's cost logic, described. Point.

Part C: and here's where it dies. The student reaches for — quote — "the Tienamen Square Protest," describing students protesting China's communist government. It's detailed. It sounds knowledgeable. And it earns *zero*.

Why? One date. Tiananmen Square was 1989. This question asks about the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The evidence is a full century outside the window. College Board's commentary is blunt: the event is outside the time period of the prompt, so no point.

[VISUAL: A timeline appears. The question's window — 1800s to early 1900s — lights up. "1989" drops in, a century to the right, with a red X.]

This is the most painful loss on the page. The student clearly *knew* Chinese history. They just grabbed the wrong century's history. Two out of three — and the lost point had nothing to do with effort.

## LIVE GRADING: SAMPLE THREE — 1 OUT OF 3

Part A: the student says the rise of India's tea industry devastated Chinese tea farmers. Thin, but it *is* the author's argument. Point.

Part B: now the wheels come off. The student claims Britain taxed tea so people bought less, and — quote — that "through the silk road chinas trade routs... had bandets making it had to import and export goods." Two fatal problems: the Silk Road is centuries outside the time period, and the tea-tax story isn't in the passage and isn't historically accurate for this situation. College Board's commentary: zero.

Part C: the student writes, roughly, that many countries stopped doing deals with China because of its political situation. That's not evidence — that's a vibe. No event, no date, nothing a grader can verify. Zero.

Final score: 1 out of 3. And notice — College Board explicitly says spelling and grammar errors don't cost points; these responses are treated as first drafts. Every point lost here was lost on *content*, not prose.

## THE MISS PATTERN

So what does this question reveal about how students bleed points on this type? Three wounds, and Part C is the deepest:

**Wound one: right knowledge, wrong century.** Sample two knew real Chinese history — just not *this question's* history. Before you write a single word of outside evidence, check the date window in the prompt. If your event can't sit on the timeline the question drew, it scores zero no matter how well you describe it.

**Wound two: vibes instead of evidence.** Sample three's "countries stopped doing deals because of the political state" names no event at all. A grader can't give a point to a feeling. If you can't name the specific event and say what happened, you don't have evidence yet.

**Wound three — the quiet one — answering A and B from your head instead of the passage.** The question tells you where the answer lives: "the author makes," "as outlined by the author." Sample three invented reasons for Part B instead of stealing them from the text. When the prompt points at the passage, the passage is the answer key.

## THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE

Here it is, and it's the whole video in one sentence:

**For A and B, the answer is in the passage — take it. For C, the answer is yours to bring — but it has to pass three checks: not in the passage, inside the time period, and specific enough to name.**

Say it back to yourself before every secondary-source question: *in the passage for A and B; outside the passage but inside the dates for C.* Miss any one of the three C checks and the point dies — just ask Sample Two, who lost it by exactly one century.

## ON YOUR EXAM — MAY 2027

Quick format note, because this is a 2025 question and your exam works differently.

Back in 2025, students got a choice about which short-answer questions to answer. On your May 2027 exam, under the new Fall 2026 format, all three SAQs are required — no more picking — and each one has a mandated source type. SAQ 1 is always a secondary text, like the tea passage you just saw. SAQ 2 is always a primary text. SAQ 3 is always a non-text source — a map, a chart, or an image.

So this exact question maps directly onto your Q1. Same historian's-passage setup, same three moves: identify their argument, describe their reason, then bring your own in-window evidence. The packaging changed; the skill didn't. Practice this shape, because it *is* your first short-answer question.
