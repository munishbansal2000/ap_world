# We Graded This: A 6/6 and a 1/6 on the Same Industrialization LEQ

**Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes (~1,000 words of narration)
**Source:** 2024 AP World History: Modern exam, Long Essay Question 3 (Set 1). Question released by College Board; student samples and scoring rationale from the official scoring commentary.

---

## COLD OPEN

[VISUAL: A red "1 / 6" stamp slams down over a page of handwritten essay.]

This student wrote a full page about factories, women, children, imperialism — and got one point. One. Not because they didn't study. Because they answered a question nobody asked.

Today we're grading three real student essays on the same 2024 question — a perfect 6, a 4, and a 1 — line by line against the rubric. You'll see exactly where each point was earned and where it died, so it doesn't happen to you.

## THE QUESTION

[VISUAL: The prompt on screen. Highlight "1750–1900," "evaluate the extent," and "economic or social change."]

"In the period circa 1750 to 1900, societies across the globe were affected by new technologies that transformed methods of production. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which the growth of industrialization led to economic or social change during this period."

What is this really asking? Two things. First: industrialization happened — that's given to you, so don't spend your essay proving it. Second: did it cause big economic or social change, or only a little? "To what extent" means you take a position on how much changed. And notice the word "or" — economic OR social. Pick one lane and stay in it. The student who scored a 6 picked social change and never left it.

[VISUAL: Rubric breakdown graphic — Thesis 1 + Context 1 + Evidence 2 + Analysis 2 = 6.]

The rubric is six points: one for a thesis that actually argues something, one for context, two for evidence, two for analysis. And that second analysis point — the complexity point — is the one almost everyone loses.

## ESSAY ONE — THE 6 OUT OF 6

[VISUAL: Essay page on screen. Each earned point gets a green check and highlight as it's called.]

First paragraph. The student writes: "The growth of industrialization led to extreme social change due to the development of a larger working/middle class... the positive effects outweighed the negative."

Thesis point — earned. It doesn't restate the prompt; it takes a position — extreme change, the positive outweighs the negative — and gives categories the essay can follow: new classes, new institutions. That's a line of reasoning.

Context point. Second sentence: the Agricultural Revolution mechanized farming and freed up workers for factory jobs. That's a broader development that happened before the industrial boom and helped cause it. Context earned — and notice, it took one sentence.

Evidence: two points. Every example is wired to the argument. The bad: child labor in British textile mills, unsafe conditions, the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire. The response: labor unions, child labor laws, fire and police departments. Multiple specific pieces, all supporting the claim. Two points, earned.

Analysis: two points. Cause and effect is the essay's skeleton — bad conditions caused unions, unions caused reforms. And the complexity point: both sides discussed, multiple effects traced, a specific disaster linked to a specific reform. Every argument is followed by its concrete effect. That's the move that gets the sixth point.

Six out of six.

## ESSAY TWO — THE 4 OUT OF 6

[VISUAL: Essay page with the crossed-out opening lines visible. Red X appears where each point is lost.]

Essay two lost two points, and both losses are instructive. The reasoning is solid: factories drew rural migrants into cities, the flood of workers let owners cut wages, the rich got richer, the poor got poorer, the gap widened. Then a second chain: cheaper mass-produced goods gave the middle class a new identity. Real cause-and-effect thinking.

So what died? First: contextualization. The essay opens with "The Industrial Revolution was characterized by a burst in technology which allowed for mass production." That sounds like context — but it's just the prompt's setup restated. The graders called it an introduction to the evidence, not a broader development. Compare essay one: the Agricultural Revolution, a specific thing that came before and caused the thing. If your "context" sentence could be swapped into the prompt without changing its meaning, it's not context — and it's worth nothing.

Second: the complexity point. The reasoning is all one lane — factories, migration, low wages, a widening gap. Every chain ends in the same place: workers had it bad. Complexity needs more than one thread: good AND bad, different groups, or a connection to another part of the world. One clean lane of causation, no matter how correct, doesn't get you there.

Total: four out of six.

## ESSAY THREE — THE 1 OUT OF 6

[VISUAL: Essay page. Slow zoom on the first paragraph.]

Now the painful one. One point out of six. Read the opening with me: the student says production wasn't growing fast enough to keep the agricultural economy going, so farmers and agricultural workers designed new methods of production — and, quote, these became what "now is call industrial revolution."

Do you see it? This whole essay explains how industrialization HAPPENED — its causes. But the prompt asks for its EFFECTS: what changed in economies and societies because of it. The entire first paragraph answers a question that wasn't asked. The graders ruled the thesis neither historically defensible nor responsive to the prompt. Zero.

The later paragraphs name real things — factories, women and children working in poor conditions, cheaper products, imperialism. That earned exactly one evidence point. But nothing is connected to an argument about change. "More products were used for trading" — okay, but what changed because of that? The essay never says. So analysis gets zero, context gets zero.

Total: one.

## THE MISS PATTERN

[VISUAL: Split screen — "CAUSES of industrialization" on the left, "EFFECTS of industrialization" on the right. An arrow tries to cross from left to right and bounces off.]

This question exposes the LEQ's most common silent killer: students flip the prompt. "Evaluate the extent to which industrialization led to change" becomes "explain how industrialization happened." The causes are what you studied first — the Agricultural Revolution, steam, coal — so under time pressure your brain reaches for the familiar material. But the rubric has no points for origins. None.

Second pattern: the complexity point is not a bonus, it's a separate skill. Essay two's chains were correct — migration, wages, the gap — and it still lost the point. Correct isn't complex. Complex means more than one thread: multiple effects, both sides, different groups, different regions.

Third: your introduction is not your context. Context is a specific broader development you describe in a sentence or two — something before, during, or continuing after the period. If it just restates the setup, it's worth nothing.

## THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE

[VISUAL: The rule appears on screen, one line at a time.]

Here is the rule: every paragraph of your LEQ must name a CHANGE. Not an origin, not a definition — a change. If you catch yourself writing about how industrialization began, stop. You are answering a different question, and the rubric has no points for it. Name the change, prove the change, explain the change. That is the whole essay.

## ON YOUR EXAM — MAY 2027

[VISUAL: "YOUR EXAM — MAY 2027" graphic. "LEQ: one broad prompt. No choice." Then: "Same rubric. Same 6 points."]

One more thing. This question is from 2024, when students got to choose one of three LEQ prompts. On your exam — May 2027 — the LEQ is a single broad prompt. No more dodging the period or the theme you don't like. The rubric is unchanged: same six points, same expectations. Which is exactly why these essays matter — with one prompt and no escape hatch, the prompt-flip mistake we just watched has nowhere to hide.
