## [0:00] COLD OPEN

Here's the painful part about this question. It's from the 2023 AP World History exam, and the students who answered it mostly *knew the history*. They could tell you who the Mamluks were. They read the passage and understood it. And they still walked away with a 1 or a 2 out of 3.

One of the samples we're about to grade together lost its point on part (c) — and what they wrote *wasn't wrong*. It was just doing the wrong job. That's the thing this video is really about: on short answer questions, the point isn't asking you for your best thoughts on the topic. It's asking for one specific move per part. Miss the move, and even a correct, thoughtful answer earns zero.

## [0:40] THE QUESTION

[VISUAL: the three-part prompt displayed on screen, parts labeled (a) (b) (c)]

The stimulus is a primary source: a passage by Ibn Khaldun, the famous Arab historian, writing in the 1300s about the Mamluks of Egypt — the dynasty of formerly enslaved soldiers who rose to rule Egypt. In the first paragraph he piles praise on them: they're divinely chosen to protect Egypt and Islam, devout converts, guardian rulers.

Then three parts, one point each:

(a) Identify ONE claim the author makes about the Mamluks in the first paragraph.

(b) Identify ONE way the passage illustrates the political situation of the Islamic world before 1450.

(c) Explain ONE way the passage could be used to illustrate differences in forms of coerced labor before 1750.

---

## [2:10] SAMPLE ONE — THE FULL-CREDIT RESPONSE

[VISUAL: Sample 2A on screen, three parts labeled]

Sample 2A got all three points. Here's how.

Part (a) asked for one claim from the first paragraph. The student wrote that the Mamluks got their authority to rule and fight from divine sources, and that they were devout Muslims. College Board says: point earned. [VISUAL: checkmark appears, first claim highlighted]

Part (b): the student said the passage implies Egypt had been divided, and the Mamluks had to restore unity — reflecting fragmentation across the Muslim world. [VISUAL: the word "fragmentation" highlighted, checkmark] That's the move for (b): take something in the passage and zoom OUT to the wider picture. Point earned.

Part (c) asked to *explain* a difference in forms of coerced labor using the passage. The student wrote that the Mamluks — despite being enslaved — were trained to read religious texts, could own land and wealth, and could even be appointed as rulers. Then came the critical half: they contrasted this with chattel slavery in the Americas, where enslaved people couldn't own property, rule, or read. [VISUAL: two columns appear — "Mamluks" vs. "Americas chattel slavery"] Point earned. Three for three.

Memorize that structure for (c): **passage example on one side, comparison on the other.** It'll matter in a minute.

## [3:40] SAMPLE TWO — THE ONE THAT LOOKS RIGHT BUT ISN'T

[VISUAL: Sample 2B on screen]

Sample 2B got two out of three — and the lost point is the most instructive thing in this whole document. Let's see why.

Part (a): the student identified that the author calls the Mamluks a gift from God with the ability to rescue Islam. Point earned. Easy.

Part (b): the student said the passage shows Islam was under attack, with Turkic and Mongol expansion threatening it. Point earned — that does illustrate the political situation.

Part (c): here it goes wrong. The student wrote that the Mamluks "were treated as slaves and imported to Egypt by the Qipchaq Turks, and when they arrived in Egypt, they were taught the way of the Qur'an, trained for combat, and paid in land and incomes."

Now — is any of that *wrong*? No. It's accurate. It's detailed. It even shows close reading. But College Board says: no point. Why?

Because part (c) doesn't ask you to describe the Mamluks' treatment. It asks you to *explain a difference in forms of coerced labor*. The student gave a description of one system and stopped. They never brought in a second form of coerced labor to differ *from*. No Americas comparison, no other example at all — so there's nothing to illustrate a "difference" with.

This is the pattern you have to see: for a comparison-style prompt, "explain" requires TWO things side by side. [VISUAL: two-column visual again, second column grayed out with "MISSING"] The student built column one beautifully and never built column two. A difference needs something to differ from.

## [5:00] SAMPLE THREE — THE ONE-POINTER

[VISUAL: Sample 2C on screen, spelling left as written]

Sample 2C got one point out of three. Quick version.

Part (a): the student wrote that the Mamluks were "sent to the muslims as a guardian ruler and devoted defenders," sent by God's grace. Point earned. Notice: even with rough spelling and shaky grammar, it earns — graders look past that unless it hides the meaning.

Part (b): the student wrote that "the turkish slaves embraced islam with the determination of true believers." No point. That's just repeating the author's claim back — it never connects to the *political situation* of the Islamic world. Zooming in when the question asks you to zoom out earns nothing.

Part (c): the student wrote that the Mamluks "were trained to use a bow and a sword," meaning they'd fight when needed. No point — no second form of coerced labor, so no difference is illustrated. They answered "what did they do?" instead of "how was this different from something else?"

## [6:00] THE MISS PATTERN

So here's what this question reveals about how students typically bleed points on this question type — the "use the passage to show X" part:

**Mistake one: answering a description question when you were asked an explanation question.** On part (c), nearly every lost point came from students describing the Mamluks really well and never comparing them to anything. If the prompt has the word "differences" or "similarities" in it, you are legally required — by the laws of points — to name two things.

**Mistake two: staying inside the passage when the question asks you to step outside it.** Parts (b) and (c) both demand you leave the document. The student who just repeated a claim got nothing on (b); the ones who connected it to fragmentation or Mongol threats got the point. Your outside knowledge is the other half of the answer.

---

## [6:45] THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE

If you remember one sentence from this video, make it this one:

**For every part of an SAQ, ask yourself: "what's the second thing?"** In (a), the second thing is the author's claim — found in paragraph one. In (b), it's the bigger political picture the passage points to. In (c), it's the other system of coerced labor you're comparing against. One part, one point, one specific move — and never leave the second thing out.

Quick format note, since this is a 2023 question. Under the old format, this was SAQ 2 out of a set with a choice. The old pick-one-of-two choice is gone.

That means this exact question — a primary-text SAQ with a claim-identification part, a context part, and a comparison part — maps directly onto your Q2. Same three-point structure, one point per part, earned independently. The graders' expectations we just walked through are the same ones waiting for you. The only real difference: in 2023 you could dodge a question like this. In 2027 you can't.
