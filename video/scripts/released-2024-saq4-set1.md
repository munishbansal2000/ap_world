## COLD OPEN

Here's something that should bother you. A student sat down for the 2024 AP World exam, answered a three-part question, and on one part wrote something completely true — and got zero points for it. Not half-wrong. True. Zero.

Meanwhile, another student wrote about nine words on a different part and got full credit.

Same exam, same question. The difference wasn't knowledge. It was one missing move — the move students miss most on this kind of question. Let's grade it together.

**[VISUAL: The three-part question on screen, clean typography. Parts (a), (b), (c) labeled.]**

## THE QUESTION

This is 2024 Short-Answer Question 4, no stimulus — just the prompt, about European imperialism in Asia, circa 1800–1914. Part (a): identify one way Asians resisted Western imperialism. Part (b): explain one way European imperialism changed Asian cultures. Part (c): explain one way imperialism in Asia changed the global economy.

One point each, three total — and each is earned independently, so blowing one part can't sink the others.

## WHAT EACH PART IS REALLY ASKING

Part (a) is the lowest bar on the exam. "Identify" means: name one real way, with enough detail that it's actually a thing. "Rebellions" plus a name — done.

Parts (b) and (c) say "explain" — and that's where the points leak. "Explain" doesn't mean "say something true about the topic." It means show the how or the why. Part (c) is the hardest version: it asks you to chain imperialism in Asia to the entire global economy, and that second link is the step students keep skipping.

**[VISUAL: "SAMPLE 4A" — student response on screen. Score revealed after grading: 3/3.]**

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 4A: 3 OUT OF 3

First sample: the perfect paper. Let's see what earned it.

Part (a): the student wrote that Asians resisted by "creating rebellions" — then named the Sepoy Rebellion against the British in India and the Boxer Rebellion in China. Point. One claim, two names.

Part (b): Vietnamese people learning French through French-run schools — new language, new education system — plus Christian missionaries spreading the religion in China after the Opium War. A cultural change, caused by imperialism, spelled out. Point.

Part (c): the money sentence — India's textile industry "collapsed due to massive British imports." British cloth floods in, India's industry collapses: a global-economy change with the mechanism attached. Point.

And notice: this student also wrote "mercantilist policies" — the wrong term for nineteenth-century British trade. College Board calls it a "read-through error" and gave the point anyway. One wrong word doesn't kill a right answer if the core claim holds up.

**[VISUAL: "SAMPLE 4B" — response on screen. Score revealed after grading: 2/3.]**

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 4B: 2 OUT OF 3

Second sample. Three sentences total. Watch what happens.

Part (a): "Asian countries resisted imperialism by increasing their industrialization, such as the Meiji Restoration." Point. Japan modernizing to fend off the West counts as resistance.

Part (b): one sentence — "European imperialism promoted the spread of Christianity in Asian countries." Point! Nine words, full credit — because it contains the link: imperialism caused Christianity to spread in Asia. Cultural change, cause attached.

Part (c): "European imperialism in Asia led to increased trade of luxury goods, such as silk." No point. College Board's exact words: this "does not explain a change in the global economy."

The statement isn't wrong — the luxury trade did grow. But the question didn't ask "name something traded." It asked for a CHANGE in the global economy. More silk trade is a fact floating in space: it never says what changed, or how, or for whom. Something true, written down, earning nothing — because the "because" is missing.

**[VISUAL: "SAMPLE 4C" — response on screen. Score revealed after grading: 1/3.]**

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 4C: 1 OUT OF 3

Third sample. This student wrote the most — nearly a full page — and scored the least: one out of three. Length is not points.

Part (a): the student listed conflicts — the Opium Wars, the Boxer Rebellion — as Asian resistance. Point. Real conflicts named as resistance clears the bar.

Part (b): imperialism changed Asian cultures by "showing them fear or in other words no mercy." No point — College Board is blunt: that "is not a cultural change due to imperialism." Fear is a feeling, "no mercy" a mood. The question asked what CHANGED in the culture — language, religion, schooling, customs. The student answered how imperialism felt, not what it altered.

Part (c): Europeans were "forcing Asians to migrate" to Australia and other parts of Asia. The painful part: College Board says that claim is correct. But the official commentary adds the killer line — the response "does not explain how this migration contributed to changes in the global economy." The student named the thing and stopped. Never said what it did to the economy. No mechanism, no point.

**[VISUAL: "THE PATTERN" — three bullet failure modes appear one by one.]**

## THE MISS PATTERN

So where's the blood? Both part-(c) answers lost — and both failed part-(b) answers lost the same way. Three ways students bleed on "explain" questions:

One — the true fact with no link. Silk trade, migration: real history, zero connection drawn to what was asked. If your sentence is true but the reader can't see HOW it answers the prompt, it's not an explanation yet.

Two — answering the wrong question. "No mercy" instead of a cultural change. Re-read the last five words of the prompt before you move on. That's where the actual target lives.

Three — stopping one step early. Part (c) needs two links: imperialism did X, which changed the global economy by Y. Students keep giving link one and walking away. Sample 4C gave you migration and never told you what it did to the economy. The whole point, left on the table.

**[VISUAL: Big on-screen text — "FOR EVERY EXPLAIN: WRITE THE BECAUSE."]**

## THE ONE RULE

The rule that fixes all three: for every "explain" part, write the because. Your answer needs the thing AND the change it caused, in one breath: "X happened, which meant Y changed, because Z."

Test yourself: can you find the "which led to" in your sentence? Sample 4A's winner had it — imports flood in, which led to India's industry collapsing. Sample 4B's silk line didn't — increased silk trade... which led to... nothing written. Can't point to the "led to"? The point isn't there yet. Write it, then check it. Every time.

## ON YOUR EXAM (MAY 2027) — WHAT CHANGED

One more thing — your exam is different. In 2024, SAQ 4 was a no-stimulus question and you chose: answer 3 or 4, your pick. That choice is gone.

On your May 2027 exam, all three SAQs are required, each with a mandated source type: Q1 a secondary text, Q2 a primary text, and Q3 ALWAYS a non-text source — a map, chart, or image. So this exact question can't appear as-is. Its closest descendant is Q3: same identify-and-explain moves, but starting from a visual — a map of European spheres of influence in China, say, or a chart of Indian textile exports. Read the visual, then write the because. The skill is identical; only the starting line moved.
