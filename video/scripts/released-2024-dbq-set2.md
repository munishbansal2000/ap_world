Here's the painful thing about this question. One student used all seven documents correctly — and scored a 4 out of 7. Another described every document accurately — and scored a 2. Both bled points anyway. Today we're grading both essays live against the real 7-point rubric, so you can see exactly where each point was won or lost.

Question from the 2024 AP World History exam, released by College Board.

[VISUAL: Prompt card on screen.]

The prompt is one sentence: "Evaluate the extent to which economic motives were the leading cause of Japanese imperialism in the period circa 1900–1945." So your job is to take a position on how far economics drove Japan's empire-building — and defend it. Seven documents: a Japanese business journal pushing for war with Russia over trade, a Japanese writer arguing the West is shutting Japan out of colonies, a Korean-American manifesto against the invasion of Manchuria, a Japanese officer's plan to seize Manchuria, an American journalist on Japan's hunger for Chinese resources, a Japanese economist warning that controlling North China won't pay off, and a photo of Japanese language classes in occupied Singapore. Some push economics, some push strategy and culture, two push back entirely. The prompt says "to what EXTENT" — so the strongest answers weigh economics against the other motives, not just list them.

[VISUAL: Rubric ladder — Thesis 1, Contextualization 1, Evidence 3, Sourcing 1, Complexity 1. Total 7.]

Quick refresher on what we're grading against. One point each for thesis and contextualization. Three for evidence — describing at least three documents, using at least four to support an argument, and one piece of knowledge from outside the documents. Two for analysis: sourcing at least two documents, and complexity. Seven total. Let's grade.

[VISUAL: Split screen — essay 1B on left, running score tally on right, starting 0/7.]

Essay one. Four out of seven. Walk it with me.

Thesis — yes. The writer says the economic motives came down to three things: lack of resources, rivalry with other powers, trade routes. Rough English, but it takes a position and gives it categories. That's all a thesis needs. Point.

Contextualization — yes. A paragraph on Japan's position before all this: trailing industrialized rivals, few trading ports, limited resources. Bigger picture, relevant. Point.

Evidence, worth three. First point: describe at least three documents. This writer describes all seven — Russian taxes at Port Arthur, England blocking its colonies, Japan's need for Chinese coal and steel, the strategic case for Manchuria, the classroom photo. Point, easily.

Second point: use at least four documents to SUPPORT AN ARGUMENT. Wording matters here. Documents 1 and 2 support the trade-pressure claim, document 5 the coal-and-steel claim, documents 6 and 4 the great-power rivalry claim. Five documents, each doing work. Point. But documents 3 and 7 are described, never linked to any argument — College Board says so flatly. Describing is not supporting. It costs this writer nothing, because they already had five. It will cost the next writer everything.

Third point: one piece of outside knowledge. The writer brings in nothing — not one fact the documents didn't hand them. Point lost.

[VISUAL: Tally at 4/7. Red X's on "Beyond docs," "Sourcing," "Complexity."]

Sourcing — no point. The writer gestures at the background of a few documents, but every attempt just repeats the attribution line. Saying a photo shows a 1943 language class, when the caption already says it's a 1943 photo, is not sourcing. Sourcing means explaining why the author's position, purpose, situation, or audience matters to your argument — the "so what." This writer never reaches the "so what."

Complexity — no point. There's a gesture at a bigger-picture move near the end, too vague to count.

Final: four out of seven. Thesis, context, two evidence points. The writer used all seven documents and walked out with barely half — because describing documents and arguing with them are two different jobs.

[VISUAL: Transition — essay 1C on screen, tally resets to 0/7.]

Essay two. Two out of seven. This one hurts more, because the writer clearly read everything.

Thesis — no point. The opening says economic motives were the main cause, "however, nationalism played a major role also." Sounds two-sided, right? College Board says no: naming a second factor is not a line of reasoning. The writer never says WHY nationalism mattered or HOW it weighed against economics. A list of factors without reasons is just a list. Point lost — and the essay has no spine.

Contextualization — yes. European colonies, a rising Russia, a world run by trade. Enough. Point.

Evidence: all seven documents described accurately — the Russian taxes, the Manchuria plan, the raw-materials race, the classroom photo. First evidence point, earned. Second point — using documents to support an argument — lost. Because there is no argument. The essay moves document by document, summarizing each in turn, never connecting one to a claim. College Board's verdict is exact: "the response does not attempt to connect any of the documents to an argument, instead it only summarizes all of the documents." Seven documents used. One evidence point. This is the most expensive misunderstanding on the DBQ: the task is not to show you read the documents. It's to show what the documents prove.

Sourcing — nothing attempted. Outside evidence — nothing attempted. Complexity — nothing attempted. Two out of seven.

[VISUAL: Both score sheets side by side. The three lost categories highlight: sourcing, complexity, beyond-docs.]

Now the miss pattern — and this question is a perfect snapshot of how students bleed nationally. The latest Chief Reader data says the three worst DBQ components are sourcing at 0.39 out of 1, complexity at 0.15 out of 1, and evidence beyond the documents at 0.47 out of 1. Our two essays earned none of the three. Essay one tried sourcing and did the thing that fails most often — restating the caption instead of giving the "so what." Neither brought a single fact from outside the documents: a full point left on the table. And complexity, the rarest point on the whole exam, needs an actual argument to live in — neither essay had one.

So the pattern: students pour their energy into the documents and skip the three moves that turn documents into a DBQ — connecting each document to a claim, sourcing two of them with a real "so what," and writing down one thing they already know.

[VISUAL: The rule appears as text, word by word.]

Here's the one rule. Every document you touch has to do a job: it proves one claim in your argument. Describe it in one breath — then finish this sentence: "This shows that..." If you can't finish that sentence, you haven't argued. You've summarized. And a summary, no matter how accurate, is a book report, not a DBQ.
