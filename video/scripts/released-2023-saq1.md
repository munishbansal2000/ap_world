# We Graded This: 2023 AP World SAQ1 (Empires & Nationalism)

- **Question:** 2023 AP World History: Modern SAQ 1 (secondary-text stimulus)
- **Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes
- **Narration:** ~1,000 words
- **Attribution:** Question from the 2023 AP World History exam, released by College Board.

## [0:00] COLD OPEN — HOOK

[VISUAL: three student scores appearing on screen: 3/3, 2/3, 1/3]

Here's the painful truth about the short-answer section. The student who scored a 1 out of 3 on this question knew plenty of history. They wrote about Ghana, about forced labor, about the colonies. And the student who got a perfect 3 out of 3 wrote sentences with spelling errors. What separated them wasn't knowledge. It was aim. Let's grade them together, line by line, and I'll show you exactly where each point was earned — and where each one leaked away.

## [0:45] THE QUESTION ON SCREEN

[VISUAL: prompt text on screen]

The question gives you a passage from a historian arguing two things: first, that European colonial empires kept their grip mostly because subject peoples accepted colonial rule as long as they saw some benefit in it — not because the empires ruled by brute force. And second, that once nationalism gave those peoples a vision of a better future, the empires collapsed surprisingly fast.

Then it asks three things. Part A: identify one claim the author makes. Part B: name one development from the second half of the twentieth century that could support the author's claim about nationalism, and explain the connection. Part C: name one development from the nineteenth or twentieth century that could challenge a claim from the first paragraph, and explain the connection.

## [1:50] WHAT EACH PART IS REALLY ASKING

[VISUAL: three-part rubric, one point each]

Three parts, three independent points. Part A is a reading check: did you actually read what the author said, or are you reaching into your own head? Parts B and C are about taking your outside knowledge and aiming it at a claim — one to support it, one to challenge it. Each one has a date window baked in. And here's the detail most students miss: for Parts B and C, naming the example isn't enough. You have to state the connection — this supports the claim because, or this challenges the claim because. The graders can't give you credit for a connection you thought but never wrote.

## [2:40] LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE ONE: THE PERFECT SCORE

[VISUAL: Sample 1A response with rubric annotations]

Let's start with the student who got all three points. For Part A, they wrote that empires maintained their rule "through acceptance... not by using force," adding that subjects stayed subjugated as long as they saw benefits. That's the author's claim, stated back from the passage. One point.

Part B: they named the African independence movements, got specific — Ghana, Nkrumah, Pan-Africanism — and tied it to the nationalist claim. College Board's own commentary says that earned the point because it connects a specific ideology to the nationalism argument. One point.

Part C: they pointed to the Belgian Congo, arguing Belgium ruled by force, extracting rubber violently — which directly contradicts the passage's claim that empires ruled by acceptance rather than force. One point. Three for three.

And look at the writing. There are grammar slips everywhere. The grader didn't care. These are first drafts under time pressure. Get the history right and state the connection, and the points land.

## [4:20] LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE TWO: THE 2 OUT OF 3

[VISUAL: Sample 1B response with rubric annotations; point (b) marked lost]

Now the student who lost one point. Part A: they identified the same claim — empires rule through acceptance, not force. Point earned.

Part B is where it breaks. They chose the Mexican Revolution and described it as an organized, courageous, effective uprising. That description is fine. But the prompt said the second half of the twentieth century, and the Mexican Revolution was in the 1910s. One wrong decade, one lost point. The example wasn't bad — it just didn't fit the window. The date qualifier in the prompt is a lock, and you have to turn the key.

Part C: they brought in the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan — the Soviets used force and threats to gain power and legitimacy, which challenges the passage's claim that empires didn't maintain rule by force. Point earned. Final score: two out of three, and the lost point had nothing to do with how much history this student knew.

## [5:40] LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE THREE: THE 1 OUT OF 3

[VISUAL: Sample 1C response with rubric annotations; parts (a) and (c) marked lost]

This is the one that hurts. Part A: the student wrote that European empires depended heavily on their colonies as a source of power. That is historically true. But College Board's commentary is blunt: it's not a claim made in the passage. The question asked for the author's claim, not a true statement. This student answered from their own knowledge instead of from the source. No point.

Part B earned the point — Ghana's independence movement, a government focused on independent development, supporting the claim that resistance needs a vision of a better future.

Part C: the student described how colonized peoples were forced into a lower class and exploited for labor and resources. All true. But then they just... stopped. They never connected it back to the passage's claim that empires ruled by acceptance rather than force. No stated connection, no point. Final score: one out of three.

## [6:50] THE MISS PATTERN

[VISUAL: three lost points, three one-line diagnoses]

Step back and look at the three points that were lost across these two responses. None of them was lost because the student lacked facts. Every single one was a miss on aim.

First: the date window. The Mexican Revolution was real, nationalist, and dramatic — and completely outside the years the prompt allowed. Before you write a single word for Parts B and C, check the date range.

Second: the source check. Part A asks what the author claims, not what's true. If your answer doesn't come from the passage, it can't earn the point, even if your history teacher would love it.

Third: the missing connection. The graders can't grade the thought in your head. "This challenges the claim because" has to appear on the page, or the point doesn't exist.

## [7:30] THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE

So here's your one rule for every SAQ you ever write: answer the source in front of you. For the reading part, your evidence comes from the passage — not from your notes. For the evidence parts, your example has to fit the date window, and you have to spell out the connection. Name it, date-check it, connect it. That's the whole game.

## [8:00] ON YOUR EXAM (MAY 2027) — FORMAT BRIDGE

[VISUAL: old format vs. new format side-by-side]

One last thing, because this is a 2023 question and your exam is different. Under the old format, there were four short-answer questions and you picked three to answer. On your exam, there are three SAQs, you answer all three, and each one has a fixed source type: Q1 uses a secondary text — exactly like the passage you just saw — Q2 uses a primary text, and Q3 uses a non-text source like a map, chart, or image. The no-stimulus question is gone entirely.

So here's the good news: a question shaped exactly like this one is guaranteed to appear on your exam as SAQ 1. The skill you just watched being graded — read a historian's argument, then aim outside knowledge at it with a stated connection — isn't old-format trivia. It's the first thing you'll do on test day. And now you know exactly where the points leak.
