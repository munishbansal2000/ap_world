## THE QUESTION (0:40–1:20)

[VISUAL: prompt text on screen]

This is Long Essay Question 2 from the 2024 AP World History exam. The prompt:

"In the period circa 1200–1750 networks of exchange led to the spread of religions, cultures, ideas, and traditions in many parts of Afro-Eurasia. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which exchange networks contributed to social or cultural change in Afro-Eurasia during this period."

## WHAT THE PROMPT IS REALLY ASKING (1:20–2:10)

Notice what the question is NOT asking. It's not asking you to describe trade routes. It's asking you to **evaluate the extent to which** exchange networks caused social or cultural change. Extent means: how much? For whom? Mostly, or only a little?

The six points are thesis, contextualization, two evidence points, and two analysis points — and every point is earned independently. You can win the thesis point and lose everything else — and we're about to watch it happen.

## LIVE GRADING — ESSAY 1: THE 6/6 (2:10–3:50)

[VISUAL: essay 1 intro on screen, thesis sentence highlighted]

Essay one scores a perfect 6 out of 6. Watch where each point lands.

**Thesis — point.** The student writes: "Because exchange networks such as the Indian Ocean and Silk Road created cultural diffusion, facilitated the spread of religions such as Buddhism and Islam, and spread government ideals through trade such as the civil service exam, exchange networks contributed to social and cultural change in Afro-Eurasia to a high degree." Three categories — diffusion, religion, government ideals — plus an extent claim ("to a high degree"). One sentence does the whole job.

**Contextualization — point.** In the same opening, the student names the networks dominating the period — the Atlantic, the Indian Ocean basin, the Silk Roads — and the great empires of the era, placing the question inside a bigger picture.

**Evidence — two points.** Every fact is hooked to the argument: Southeast Asia turning Buddhist, Mansa Musa converting to Islam, Sufi mystics spreading Islam along trade routes, Vietnam and Japan adopting the civil service exam, Russia imitating European dress and governance. That's not a list — it's evidence working for a claim.

**Analysis and complexity — the final two points.** Cause and effect, spelled out — the trans-Saharan network *led to* Mansa Musa's conversion; tributary relations *led to* Vietnam and Japan changing. More than four examples pushing one nuanced argument — that's the complex understanding the rubric rewards. Six for six.

## LIVE GRADING — ESSAY 2: THE 4/6 (3:50–5:10)

[VISUAL: essay 2 opening sentence on screen, annotated]

Essay two is the painful one. Two full pages. Plenty of facts. It scores 4 out of 6 — and the two lost points are the whole lesson.

**Thesis — zero.** The opening claim: "Exchange networks heavily changed any society it interacted with." College Board: that just restates the prompt. Adding the word "heavily" is not an argument — it's the prompt in different clothes. Without a reason, categories, or any extent evaluation, the point is gone. From a two-page essay. The cheapest point on the rubric, sitting right there in sentence one.

**Contextualization — point.** The student names the Indian Ocean trade, the Silk Road, the trans-Saharan network. Specific and relevant.

**Evidence — two points.** The spice trade, new maritime technology, the Silk Road spreading Islam, the triangular trade between Europe, Africa, and the Americas. Specific facts, attached to the argument.

**Analysis — one point, complexity — zero.** The student explains how the Indian Ocean trade connected sea- and land-based empires, and how Silk Road merchants spread religion. That's reasoning. But the essay never steps back — no second look, no nuance. College Board: the evidence wasn't used to support a complex argument. That's the second lost point.

## LIVE GRADING — ESSAY 3: THE 2/6 (5:10–6:00)

[VISUAL: essay 3 full page on screen, monsoon paragraph highlighted]

Essay three scores 2 out of 6. It's short, and it shows what happens when you describe trade instead of arguing change.

**Thesis — point.** Yes, really. "The silk road, Indian Ocean, and the tran-Sahra trade help spread religion and culture." College Board calls it minimally acceptable. Short, unglamorous, point earned — and the two-page essay didn't manage it.

**Contextualization — zero.** The opening just restates the prompt's setup.

**Evidence — one of two.** Real facts: the Silk Roads focused on luxury goods, Indian Ocean merchants learned to use monsoon winds. Specific and relevant — one point. But the second point needs evidence *supporting an argument*, and there's no argument here.

**Analysis — zero.** College Board's killer line: the student discusses monsoon winds "but does not explain the effect of how this more efficient method of travel resulted in social or cultural change." The essay answers "how did trade work" — the prompt asks "what did trade change." Wrong question, zero reasoning points.

## THE MISS PATTERN (6:00–6:40)

[VISUAL: three bullet points appearing one by one]

Three ways this question bleeds points:

**One: the reworded-prompt thesis.** The 4-point essay lost its thesis point on a sentence that just repeated the prompt with stronger adjectives. A thesis needs an actual position — an extent claim, a reason, or categories like the 6-point essay's split between religions and government ideals.

**Two: facts without a job.** The 2-point essay had real knowledge — monsoon winds, luxury goods, trade cities. But nothing tied those facts to *social or cultural change*, the actual question. A fact on the LEQ earns you nothing until you connect it to your claim. Describe less, argue more.

**Three: one-note analysis.** The 4-point essay's reasoning was fine — every paragraph said "this network caused this change." But it never stepped back: different effects in different places, multiple angles, many examples woven into one nuanced argument. That's the last point, and it's the difference between a good essay and a great one.

## THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE (6:40–7:00)

If you take one rule into your LEQ: **argue change, not trade.** The prompt doesn't ask what the trade routes were — it asks what they changed. Before you write a sentence, ask: does this sentence say what changed? If it only says what was traded, where, or how, it's a fact without a job. Chain every fact to a claim about change, and the points follow.
