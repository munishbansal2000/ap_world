**THE QUESTION**

Here's the prompt: "Evaluate the extent to which communist rule transformed Soviet and/or Chinese societies in the period circa 1930 to 1990." That's it. One sentence.

You get seven documents, spanning 1928 to 1983 — a Soviet poster about workers' evening schools, a prison memoir from Stalin's purges where a son denounces his father under torture, a 1946 essay praising state support for Soviet mothers, a 1957 Chinese article where local leaders openly criticize the Party's absolute power, a 1958 Party report on famine and food riots quietly blaming local officials, a 1973 article by a Chinese woman in the Party on women's incomplete gains, and a 1983 Soviet piece attacking capitalist reforms.

Notice what the packet hands you: real change — schools, women's rights — right next to repression, famine, and corruption. The question isn't "did communism change things." It's asking you to measure: how much, in what ways, and what stayed the same. That's the word "extent" — and your thesis needs categories: what changed, what didn't, and why.

**LIVE GRADING: ESSAY ONE**

Essay one. I'm grading this exactly the way College Board did. Watch the tracker.

Thesis. The student writes — and this is a direct quote — "Communist rule transformed Soviet and Chinese societies significantly in the period circa 1930-1990."

That's the prompt with "significantly" bolted on — a restatement, no point. A thesis earns its point only with a reason or categories of argument. "Significantly" does neither. Tracker: zero.

Contextualization. The student's attempt: "Communists believe that economy is controlled by the government and not privately owned." One sentence — a dictionary definition. College Board: no point. That's a passing phrase, not context. Context has to be actual history. Something like: the communists seized power through the Russian Revolution and the Chinese Civil War, promising to tear down the old order. That's the whole bar. Tracker: still zero.

Evidence. Now the painful part. This student correctly described six documents — that's the first evidence point. Then they connected five of those documents to real arguments — women's lives changing, people being mistreated, Chinese society suffering through food shortages. That's the second point. Two out of three. The third evidence point goes to knowledge from outside the packet — and this student never even tried. Zero on that row.

Sourcing: no attempt. Zero. Complexity: no attempt. Zero.

Final score: two out of seven.

[VISUAL: Tracker fills to 2/7]

Six documents handled correctly, and the essay dies at two — the thesis was a copy of the prompt, the context was a definition, and not one document's motive was explained or outside fact used.

**LIVE GRADING: ESSAY TWO**

Essay two. Same packet, more to show.

Thesis: earned. It's clunky — women, stronger governments, negative effects on wellbeing — but it sets up categories, so it counts. Point.

Contextualization: earned. The student described communism's goal of collective ownership and how communist rule was established in Russia. And note this: the first sentence has a flat-out chronological error — College Board gave the point anyway, because the rest of the context was accurate. Your overall picture counts, not your trivia. Point.

Evidence: three out of three. Six documents described and six tied to arguments — women's opportunities, state overreach, ordinary people suffering. Plus the outside fact: Mao's Great Leap Forward policies caused the famine in that 1958 report. Full row. Tracker: five of seven.

Now the two that got away.

Sourcing: zero. The student sourced one document — the Xia Ping article, written to inspire Chinese women — but you need two. And the second attempt stings. Writing about the prison memoir, they said the point of view was "important" because the author showed overwhelming emotion — then just described the scene. College Board's ruling: describing the document is not sourcing. One more sentence would have done it — this author was a Party member who lived through the prisons, so his account is eyewitness evidence that the terror reached into families. Same knowledge. One sentence missing. Point gone.

Complexity: zero. The student listed positives and negatives — women gained, the state seized control — but College Board says it "lacks the sophistication and analysis." A list of good and bad things is not complexity. Complexity is friction: women gained real rights under communism AND were still expected to carry the household alone — that's the Kollontai essay and the Xia Ping article arguing with each other. Hold both truths in one sentence, and you earn it.

Final score: five out of seven.

**THE MISS PATTERN**

Zoom out — these two essays aren't outliers. They're the pattern.

[VISUAL: Bar chart — Sourcing 0.39/1, Complexity 0.15/1, Evidence beyond the documents 0.47/1]

College Board's 2025 Chief Reader data says it plainly: sourcing averaged 0.39 out of 1, complexity 0.15, evidence beyond the documents 0.47 — the three worst components on the whole DBQ.

Essay one knew six documents and scored two — skipping the thesis, context, sourcing, outside-knowledge, and complexity moves. Essay two was perfect on evidence, three for three, and still lost both analysis points.

So the miss pattern is not "students don't know the history." It's that students treat the documents like a summary assignment — describe, describe, describe — and never make the two moves that turn description into analysis: explain why the source's position matters, and hold two competing truths at once.

[VISUAL: Takeaway card]

**THE TAKEAWAY**

Here's your one rule. Write it down. For every document you touch, answer two questions in the same breath: what does it show, and why does it matter to my argument.

Description without connection got our first student a two. Connection without sourcing got our second student a five. Do both and you're playing for six or seven — the documents are free points; the thinking is where the exam is won.
