## THE QUESTION ON SCREEN (0:45–2:00)

**[VISUAL: the SAQ 1 prompt appears, parts a/b/c highlighted one at a time]**

The prompt has three parts, one point each. It asks you to: "Identify ONE argument that the author makes in the first paragraph. Identify ONE piece of evidence that the author uses to support an argument in the passage." And part c: "Explain how ONE development in the period 1200–1750 could be used to support the author's argument in the second paragraph about history writing and art creation legitimizing states' exercise of power."

The stimulus is a secondary source — a passage by the historian Chase F. Robinson. In plain terms, it says: in the period roughly 1200 to 1750, Muslims wrote a *mountain* of history — court chronicles, biographies of Muhammad and religious figures — far more than Jewish or Christian writers of the time. And Robinson's second-paragraph argument is that rulers used this history writing, plus art and architecture, to make their power look legitimate.

**[VISUAL: three labeled boxes — (a) Find the claim / (b) Find his evidence / (c) Bring your own example]**

Here's what each part is really testing. Part (a): find the author's *claim* in paragraph one — that's reading comprehension. Part (b): find the *evidence the author uses* — still inside the passage. Part (c): leave the passage, reach into your own knowledge of 1200–1750, and *explain how* a development you know connects to his legitimation argument. That word "explain" is doing heavy lifting — it means how or why. Keep that in mind.

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 1A: THE 3/3 (2:00–3:30)

**[VISUAL: Sample 1A on screen, green check marks appearing per part]**

Sample 1A earned all three points. Watch *where* each point landed.

Part (a): the student wrote that Robinson "argues that the impact of Islamic historical writing significantly outweighs the literature written by Jewish or Christian authors." A claim the author actually makes. Point one.

Part (b): the student says the author supports his argument by pointing to "numerous religious biographies" of religious figures and the Prophet Muhammad, published because religious institutions wanted them. Evidence *from the passage*, quoted from it. Point two.

Part (c): the student brings the Taj Mahal — built by Shah Jahan — and here's the money line: its lavishness "was effective in legitimizing Mughal, Islamic rule in a majority Hindu state."

**[VISUAL: that sentence glows — underline "legitimizing Mughal, Islamic rule"]**

They didn't just name the Taj Mahal. They wrote the connection sentence. That's what "explain how" pays for. Three for three.

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 1B: THE 2/3 (3:30–4:45)

**[VISUAL: Sample 1B on screen — two green checks, one red X on part (c)]**

Sample 1B got the first two. Part (a): "many people were enthusiastic about the forms of history writing that reinforced traditional religious institutions" — a real claim from the passage. Point. Part (b): "people wrote many biographies of religious figures and of the Prophet" — the author's evidence. Point.

Then part (c). The student's entire answer: "One development that supports the author's argument is the Prophet of Muhammad."

**[VISUAL: the part (c) answer isolated, big on screen. Red annotation: "not a development. not explained."]**

College Board's note: naming "the Prophet of Muhammad" is not *explaining* a development. Muhammad died in 632 — the question asked for 1200–1750 — and the answer never says how anything legitimized any state's power. Zero on the last part, all because the connection sentence was never written.

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 1C: THE 1/3 (4:45–6:00)

**[VISUAL: Sample 1C on screen — one green check, two red X's]**

Now sample 1C, the painful one. Part (a): "empire building established a market for those that desired historical writing" — a claim from the passage. Point earned.

Part (b): the student's entire answer is "Prophet Muhammad."

**[VISUAL: split screen — left: "Prophet Muhammad" / right: "biographies written ABOUT the Prophet"]**

College Board's note on this one is the whole lesson: the author's evidence is about *biographies written about* the Prophet — not the person of the Prophet. Part (b) never asks "what do you know about this topic?" It asks what the *author uses*. If your part (b) answer could have been written without reading the passage at all, it almost certainly earned nothing.

Part (c): the student wrote "monumental architecture." That's it.

**[VISUAL: the words "monumental architecture" alone on screen. Then the correct rubric bullet fades in: "Rulers used religious ideas, art, and monumental architecture to legitimize their rule."]**

The heartbreaking part? "Monumental architecture" is on College Board's own list of acceptable ideas. The student was holding the right idea. But the task says *explain how*. They named the category and stopped — never wrote how it legitimized anyone's power. One more sentence — "which legitimized the ruler's power by ___" — and that's a point. Without it, it's zero.

## THE MISS PATTERN (6:00–6:50)

**[VISUAL: two-column graphic — "THE TWO BLEEDS"]**

This question reveals how students typically bleed points on source-analysis short answers, and it's two versions of the same disease:

**Bleed one — answering from memory instead of the passage.** Part (b) is a reading question wearing a history costume. The student who wrote "Prophet Muhammad" knew a fact. But the point wasn't for the fact — it was for *the author's move*, the evidence *he* used. The fix: after you write part (b), ask — "is this in the passage?" If not, it's wrong.

**Bleed two — naming instead of explaining.** Part (c) asks you to *explain how*. "Taj Mahal" gets nothing by itself. "Monumental architecture" gets nothing. "The Prophet of Muhammad" gets nothing. The point lives in the connection sentence — the "which legitimized this ruler's power because ___" sentence. Sample 1A wrote it. Samples 1B and 1C didn't. Two students had the right idea; only one wrote the sentence that earned the point.

---

## THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE (6:50–7:15)

**[VISUAL: full-screen text card]**

The point goes to the move, not the fact.

Each part names its move: identify the argument, identify the evidence, *explain* the connection. Write the move. And if you catch yourself just naming a person, a building, or an event — stop, and add the sentence that connects it to what the question asked. One more sentence. That's the difference between a 1 and a 3.

## ON YOUR EXAM — MAY 2027 (7:15–7:50)

**[VISUAL: "2024 = OLD FORMAT" stamp, then "YOUR EXAM: MAY 2027" with the new SAQ table]**

Quick format bridge, because this question is from 2024 — the old format. On your exam, the SAQ 3/4 choice is gone. All three short-answer questions are required, and each has a fixed source type: Q1 is always a secondary-text source, Q2 is always primary text, and Q3 is always a non-text source — a map, a chart, or an image.

This question maps *directly* onto your Q1. A secondary source, three parts, the same three moves: find the author's argument, find the author's evidence, then explain a development that connects. Nail what Sample 1A did here — especially that connection sentence — and you're built for Q1.
