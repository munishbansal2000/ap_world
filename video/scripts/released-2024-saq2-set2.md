## COLD OPEN

[VISUAL: Split screen — a handwritten student answer on the left, a big red "2/3" stamping in on the right]

This student knew the history. Cold. They wrote about the White Man's Burden, the civilizing mission, the Indian Revolt of 1857 — pig fat, cow fat, greased rifle cartridges, all of it. And College Board still took a point away. Not because the history was wrong — but because of one thing this student didn't do, the single most common way students bleed points on short-answer questions. We're grading this one live, with College Board's own scoring notes as judge.

## THE QUESTION ON SCREEN

[VISUAL: The 1897 photograph fades in — caption: "Graduation ceremony, Christian theological seminary, Northern India, British colonial period, 1897"]

The stimulus is a photograph: an 1897 graduation ceremony at a Christian theological seminary in Northern India. Indian graduates are seated and standing around two British missionaries in the center.

Three parts, one point each, earned independently:

(a) Identify ONE development during 1450 to 1750 that contributed to the activities shown in the image.

(b) Explain ONE reason why imperialist governments often supported activities of the type shown in the image during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

(c) Explain ONE way indigenous peoples in Africa and/or Asia resisted the spread of the cultural practices reflected in the image during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

## WHAT EACH PART IS REALLY ASKING

[VISUAL: Three-part breakdown graphic — (a) "Zoom out," (b) "Whose reason?", (c) "Pushback"]

Part (a) is a zoom-out. The photo is 1897 — but part (a) doesn't want 1897. It wants you to reach back to 1450–1750 and name something that made scenes like this one possible centuries later. That's contextualization: the background engine behind the picture.

Part (b) flips the camera. It doesn't ask why missionaries did what they did — it asks why GOVERNMENTS backed them. Whose reason? The imperial state's. Mixing the two up is where points go to die.

Part (c) wants resistance — one specific, named, dated way colonized people pushed back against this cultural pressure.

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 1: THE PAINFUL ONE (2/3)

[VISUAL: Student response on screen; rubric checklist appears on the side, checking off as we go]

First up, a response that scored 2 out of 3. Watch part (b) — the student writes that imperialist governments backed missionaries to spread Christianity and education to peoples they considered less civilized — the whole civilizing-mission idea. That is exactly the government's reason. Point earned.

Part (c): the student names the Indian Revolt of 1857 — Indian soldiers rebelling against British rule after being forced to bite rifle cartridges greased with pig and cow fat, violating Muslim and Hindu religious rules. Specific, dated, on target. Point earned.

Now part (a). The student answered with "the development of social Darwinism and the White Man's Burden" — the belief that it was a nation's duty to educate and convert societies it deemed less civilized.

True history? Yes. Decent analysis? Honestly, yes. Does it earn the point? No. Social Darwinism and the White Man's Burden are nineteenth-century ideas — and the prompt said 1450 to 1750. College Board's own scoring note says it flatly: outside the time period of the prompt, no point.

This is the painful one. The student wasn't confused. They weren't blanking. They knew the material — they used the civilizing-mission idea to earn part (b) thirty seconds later. They just didn't check the date bracket on part (a). True answer, wrong century, zero points.

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 2: FULL CREDIT (3/3)

[VISUAL: Green checkmarks stamping in, one per part]

Now the full-credit response — 3 out of 3. Notice what's different. It's not longer. It's more specific.

Part (a): instead of a big idea, the student names mechanisms — Europeans improving maritime and navigational technologies, integrating into Afroeurasian trade networks, and joint-stock companies like the British East India Company carrying Christian ideas into India. Every clause lives inside 1450–1750. Point.

Part (b): the civilizing mission again — the imperial powers' sense of moral obligation to convert indigenous peoples, religious schools, and the cultural control that came with diminishing native traditions. The government's reason, clearly stated. Point.

Part (c): the Sepoy Rebellion, sparked when those pig-and-cow-fat cartridges violated Hindu and Muslim taboos and Indian soldiers rose up armed in protest. Point.

Three points, three specific mechanisms. That's the pattern — nothing in this response is vague enough to fit a different question.

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 3: THE GENERIC ONE (1/3)

[VISUAL: Mostly red X marks; one lonely green check]

Last one — 1 out of 3, instructive in the opposite direction. Part (a): the student wrote, quote, "slavery. Slavery was a super big thing back then." Slavery was big between 1450 and 1750 — true. But it doesn't explain missionary seminaries in India. It doesn't answer the question asked. No point.

Part (b): the student wrote that imperialist governments supported missionary activity because they "need the people for harsh labor." Again — broadly true about imperialism, but it's not a reason governments supported MISSIONARIES. Wrong "why." No point.

Part (c): the student wrote that indigenous people resisted "by believing strongly in their faith and not believing in any other faith." College Board's own commentary calls this phrasing vague — but it earned the point, because underneath the vagueness it says one real thing: people resisted by refusing to adopt Christianity. That point was a gift. CB reads your response as a first draft and reads generously — but vague scraped by here exactly once. Don't build a strategy on it.

## THE MISS PATTERN

[VISUAL: "Three ways students bleed points on SAQs" — three bleed-point icons]

So here's the miss pattern this question exposes — three ways students bleed points on this question type:

One: ignoring the date bracket. Part (a) handed you 1450–1750 right in the prompt, and the most common lost point across the samples came from answering with nineteenth-century content. The dates aren't decoration — answer outside them and your knowledge doesn't count.

Two: answering the wrong "why." Part (b) asked for the government's reason, not the missionary's motive, not a general fact about imperialism. Before you write, ask: who is this part asking about?

Three: generic sentences. "Slavery was a big thing." "They needed labor." Compare that to "joint-stock companies" and "pig and cow fat in rifle cartridges." Specificity is the currency. One named detail beats three vague sentences every time.

## THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE

[VISUAL: The rule on screen, big text: "Match the dates, then name the mechanism."]

So the rule — write this one down: Match the dates, then name the mechanism.

Before you write a single word: circle the date range, circle the actor. Then give one specific, named, dated thing that actor did inside those years. If your answer could fit any century or any empire, it's not specific enough — rewrite it until it only fits this one.

## ON YOUR EXAM — MAY 2027

[VISUAL: Old format → new format diagram: "2024: SAQ2 = primary source (photo), choose SAQ 3 or 4" → "2027: Q1 secondary text · Q2 primary text · Q3 map/chart/image — all required"]

Quick bridge to YOUR exam, May 2027. This question is from 2024, back when you still chose between SAQ 3 and 4 — that choice is gone. On your exam, all three SAQs are required. And each one has a mandated source type: Q1 uses a secondary text, Q2 uses a primary TEXT, and Q3 is the non-text source — a map, chart, or image.

So a photograph like this 1897 seminary picture? On your exam it would show up on Q3, not Q2 — and Q2 would hand you a written primary source instead. What doesn't change: the moves. Part (a)'s zoom-back-in-time is still contextualization. Parts (b) and (c) still ask you to explain one cause and one resistance — one point each, earned independently.
