## COLD OPEN

[VISUAL: Title card — "We Graded This: 2025 SAQ 2"]

This question looks like a free three points. Three short parts, three independent points. But two out of three official student samples lost at least one point. One student wrote a single word for Part A and got the point. Another wrote a full paragraph for Part C and got nothing. We'll grade three real answers live — judged by College Board's own words — and the pattern is one you'll hit on your exam.

## THE QUESTION

[VISUAL: Question stem on screen. Passage paraphrase banner underneath.]

The setup is an excerpt from a German women's newspaper. The author argues that women should be educated, enjoy liberty, and be able to act independently in the state — and in one paragraph she notes the rising poverty and misery around her.

Then the three tasks: Part A — "Identify one likely audience for the claims that the author makes in the passage." Part B — "Describe one historical context during the nineteenth century that explains the increased poverty and misery referred to in the fourth paragraph." Part C — "Explain how one ideology or set of ideas likely influenced the author's claims in the passage."

Three points, each earned independently — and here's the move most students miss: each part's verb is your instruction manual. A says *identify* — just name it. B says *describe* — give it characteristics. C says *explain* — show how or why.

## WHAT EACH PART IS REALLY ASKING

[VISUAL: Rubric sidebar — IDENTIFY / DESCRIBE / EXPLAIN with plain-language definitions]

Part A is sourcing — who is she writing for? One clean noun: women. That is the whole bar.

Part B is context — what made workers miserable in the 1800s? Note the time lock: *nineteenth century*.

Part C is the heavyweight: influence. You need an ideology — feminism, liberalism, Enlightenment rights — and the arrow from that ideology to her claims. The ideology alone isn't the point. The arrow is.

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 2A: 3 OUT OF 3

[VISUAL: Sample 2A on screen, parts A/B/C highlighted. Scores pop in: 1, 1, 1.]

First response. Part A: "lower class women in Germany" — point — plus a mini-essay on Wollstonecraft and de Gouges that College Board calls way more than needed. Part B: the Industrial Revolution — low sanitation, long hours, low wages, workers flooding into cities, squalid tenements. "Much more detailed than the minimum." Part C: Enlightenment natural-rights ideas, tied directly to her language of liberty and rights for women. Point.

All three. The lesson from 2A? Overwriting doesn't lose points — but it costs time you'll need elsewhere.

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 2B: 2 OUT OF 3

[VISUAL: Sample 2B on screen. Scores pop in: 1, 1, 0 — with the 0 in red.]

Part A: oppressed women with few rights, tied to the piece appearing in a women's newspaper — point. Part B: the Industrial Revolution forcing families to migrate for work. College Board calls this "overly generalized" — and still gives the point.

Then Part C. Here's the student's answer: "One ideology or set of ideas likely influenced the author's claims in the passage by setting an example. Up to this time, many colonies acquired freedom and independence from their mother countries through methods like rebellions and revolts."

Is there an ideology in there? No. "Setting an example" is not an ideology. Colonies winning independence is an event, not a set of ideas. College Board: the response "does not identify an ideology or a set of ideas that influenced the author's arguments." Zero — not because the student wrote badly, but because they answered "what events were happening?" instead of "what ideology shaped her thinking?" Close to the neighborhood, wrong house.

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 2C: 1 OUT OF 3

[VISUAL: Sample 2C on screen. Scores pop in: 1, 0, 0.]

Part A: "women." One word. Point. The cheapest point on this exam.

Part B: the Great Depression. A real event, real poverty and misery. Zero points — because the prompt said nineteenth century, and the Depression is twentieth. College Board: "outside of the time period specified in the prompt." Two words in the question — "nineteenth century" — and the student walked right past them. Everything they wrote was true, and worthless, because it was true about the wrong century.

Part C: the student describes how women were treated unfairly — housewives, no respect, nobody cared. Accurate description of the problem — but not an answer to "how did an ideology influence her claims?" College Board says the student describes patriarchal norms well enough to count as a "set of ideas," but never explains how those ideas influenced the author. And there's a deeper problem: patriarchy didn't inspire her to demand rights — she is writing *against* it. Even the half-implied connection points the wrong way. Zero.

One out of three. The points didn't die from ignorance — they died from not matching the answer to the verb and the time frame.

## THE MISS PATTERN

[VISUAL: Side-by-side Part C answers from 2B and 2C, with red annotations.]

What do 2B and 2C share on Part C? Neither named an ideology that shaped her thinking. One hid behind "setting an example." The other described women's suffering and stopped — never explaining the influence.

That's the number-one way students lose influence questions: they describe the topic instead of drawing the arrow. "Explain how X influenced the author" has two halves — X, and the influence. Students do only one. On the context part, they name an event but forget the prompt's boundaries — wrong century, no characteristics. On identify, almost nobody loses.

The rubric's verbs are a contract: identify means name it, describe means give characteristics, explain means show the mechanism. Students who lose points aren't failing history. They're failing to read the verb.

## THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE

[VISUAL: Rule card — "Name it, then draw the arrow."]

One sentence: name it, then draw the arrow.

For any "explain how" prompt — and the new exam is full of them — naming the thing is half the move. The point lives in the connection back to the passage's claims. Finish every Part C with a "because" that ties the ideology to her words. Feminism matters here not as a vocabulary word but because her call for women's education is feminist thinking in action. Enlightenment ideas matter because her language of natural liberty comes straight from them. Name it, then draw the arrow. If your answer doesn't have a because, it's not finished.

## ON YOUR EXAM (MAY 2027)

[VISUAL: Format bridge graphic — 2025 SAQ 2 → 2027 SAQ Q2]

One thing to know: this is a 2025 question, from the old format where you chose among four SAQs. That choice is gone. On your exam all three SAQs are required, each with a mandated source type: Q1 secondary source, Q2 primary source text, Q3 non-text — a map, chart, or image.

This maps directly onto Q2: a primary text with the A/B/C trio of audience, context, and influence of ideas. You will see one built exactly like this. The verbs, the time-bound context trap, the draw-the-arrow Part C — all of it carries into May 2027.
