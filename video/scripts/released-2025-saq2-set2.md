**COLD OPEN**

What if I told you the difference between a perfect score and a one-out-of-three wasn't what the student knew — it was whether they read the right paragraph?

Today we're grading real answers to a 2025 question about Cold War Africa — three real student responses from College Board's official samples, with their official scores. One student wrote less and got all three points. Another wrote nearly a full page — and walked away with one.

Let's see exactly where each point was earned, and where it died.

## [VISUAL: The question stem and passage on screen. Passage shown but NOT read aloud — paraphrase only.]

**THE QUESTION ON SCREEN**

The setup: a writer arguing the United States should support African decolonization with a Marshall Plan-style aid package — because U.S. officials feared the Soviets would pull newly independent African nations into the communist orbit, and because European colonialism was a "discredited system" already collapsing.

Three parts. Part A: identify one reason U.S. officials were concerned about communism, as noted in the first paragraph. Part B: describe the historical situation behind the "system of colonialism" in the second paragraph. Part C: explain one way the source reflects Africa's political situation in the second half of the twentieth century.

## [VISUAL: Three labeled boxes — A = FIND it (identify), B = NARRATE it (describe), C = CONNECT it (explain).]

**WHAT EACH PART IS REALLY ASKING**

Part A is a find-and-name move: pull the specific reason out of paragraph one. The guide accepts answers like the Cold War playing out globally, fear the Soviets would court the developing world, or the domino worry — one African country goes communist, others follow.

Part B is the describe move: narrate the Scramble for Africa — the nineteenth-century carve-up of the continent by Britain, France, Germany, and others.

Part C is the explain move: connect the document to the bigger picture — U.S. and Soviet jockeying for influence, the decolonization struggles, Africa's rebuilding compared to postwar Europe.

Here's the key structural thing: Part B wants you to step *outside* the passage and bring in your knowledge. Part A wants you to stay *inside* it. That distinction is the whole game today.

## [VISUAL: Sample 1 fills the screen. Checkmarks appear as each part is graded.]

**LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 1: 3 OUT OF 3**

First up, the perfect score. For Part A, this student said U.S. officials feared communism because it clashed head-on with America's own capitalist system — government control versus free markets — and that clash made containing communism a priority.

Check. One point. A specific, named reason, tied to what the passage is about.

Part B: European states industrialized, built weapons Africa couldn't resist, colonized the continent, and drained its raw materials to feed their own factories. The Scramble, described with actual mechanics. Check.

Part C: the passage's push for U.S.-backed decolonization reflects Africa's mid-century independence movements. Check.

Three for three. Every answer names a specific thing — an ideology clash, the Scramble's mechanics, decolonization. No poetry. Just the answer.

## [VISUAL: Sample 2 on screen. Part A gets a red X; Parts B and C get checks.]

**LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 2: 2 OUT OF 3**

Now this one. Part A, in full: quote — "to prevent such a greate disaster from taking place." That's the whole answer.

Zero. Not because of the spelling — grammar doesn't count unless it blocks meaning. Zero because "disaster" is not a reason. It's a feeling. The student sensed *something bad* was supposed to happen, but never said what the U.S. was actually worried about — no Soviets, no spreading communism, no ideology. One vague word where the rubric demanded one concrete reason.

But Parts B and C both earned. Part B describes European powers colonizing Africa to grab its riches — that lands. Part C connects newly independent African states to the U.S.–Soviet fight for influence — that lands too.

Two out of three. One point lost entirely to vagueness in Part A.

## [VISUAL: Sample 3 on screen — visibly longer than the other two. "1/3" stamp fades in.]

**LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 3: 1 OUT OF 3**

And now the painful one. This student wrote the *most* — nearly a full page. But length doesn't score; the right answer scores.

Part A leaned on "equality": communism has roots in equality, wars might start... College Board's verdict: zero. Equality wasn't the reason U.S. officials gave in paragraph one — it's the student's own association with the word "communism."

Part B: the Scramble for Africa, nineteenth-century European powers invading to take land. Point earned. One check.

Part C: the student went back to equality — "concern for equality," racial equality, peace. Zero again. Because this passage is about decolonization and superpower rivalry, not racial equality. The student answered the theme in their head, not the document on the page.

Lots of writing. One point. The historical knowledge was real — the Scramble answer proves it. It just wasn't pointed at the right target.

## [VISUAL: A highlighted quote of Part A's wording: "as noted in the first paragraph of the passage." A compass pin drops on the passage.]

**THE MISS PATTERN**

Here's the bleed pattern this question exposes.

Parts A and C both punished the same habit: answering from your general associations instead of the document. Part A literally says "as noted in the first paragraph of the passage." That's not decoration — that's a set of coordinates. The students who lost points treated "identify" as an invitation to free-associate about communism: disaster... equality. The student who got the point simply reached into the paragraph and pulled out the reason sitting there: the ideology clash, containment, the domino fear.

Same on Part C. "Explain one way the *source* reflects" the political situation — the source is about decolonization and Cold War rivalry. "Equality for all the races" is the student's theme, not the document's.

General knowledge is for Part B. Parts A and C are about the passage — work it like evidence.

## [VISUAL: One sentence on a clean card.]

**THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE**

If the question points at the passage, the answer lives in the passage.

"As noted in the first paragraph" means your job is to find it there and name it — one specific, concrete reason. Vague is not a reason. Your feelings about communism are not a reason. And your favorite theme is not the document's theme. The paragraph is the evidence; everything else is a wish.

## [VISUAL: "YOUR EXAM — MAY 2027" graphic. Three boxes: Q1 = secondary text, Q2 = primary text, Q3 = non-text source.]

**ON YOUR EXAM (MAY 2027) — THE FORMAT BRIDGE**

Quick bridge, because this is a 2025 question under the old format, and your exam looks different.

In 2025, SAQ 2 was a primary-source question like this one, and there was a choice between two different SAQ 3s. Both are gone for you. On the May 2027 exam, all three SAQs are required — no choices — and the source types are assigned: Q1 is a secondary text, Q2 is a primary text, Q3 is a non-text source — a map, a chart, or an image.

So this 2025 question is exactly the animal you'll meet as your Q2. Same identify/describe/explain rhythm, same off-document bleed pattern. Practice it this way now: when a part cites a paragraph, read that paragraph, and answer from that paragraph.
