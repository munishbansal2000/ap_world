## 1. COLD OPEN — THE HOOK (0:00–0:45)

[VISUAL: Title card — "We graded this." Cut to the three scores stacked: 3, 2, 1.]

Here's something nobody tells you about short-answer questions: the difference between full credit and zero credit is almost never how much you *know*. It's whether you respected the verb in the prompt.

This is a 2025 AP World SAQ — revolutions, 1750 to 1900. Three parts, three points, no stimulus. Three real student responses from College Board's released samples: one scored a perfect 3, one scored a 1, and the middle one? A full, confident paragraph — and it lost a point for a reason most students would never catch.

Today we're grading all three live against College Board's own scoring notes: exactly where each point was earned, and where it slipped away.

## 2. THE QUESTION (0:45–1:30)

[VISUAL: The question on screen, all three parts, clean text.]

Three parts. A: identify one factor behind the outbreak of revolutions, about 1750 to 1900. B: explain one way revolutionary movements *used ideologies* to change societies in that period. C: explain one way they were *challenged* while trying.

Three verbs: **identify**, then **explain**, then **explain** again. That distinction is the entire video. Identifying means naming one real factor. Explaining means showing the *how* or the *why* — the mechanism, not just the label. Remember that, because two of the three students we're about to grade tripped on exactly that word.

## 3. LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 4A: THE PERFECT 3 (1:30–3:00)

[VISUAL: Sample 4A on screen, left. Rubric checklist on the right, points checking off one by one.]

First up, Sample 4A — 3 for 3. Quick, because there's nothing to fix.

[VISUAL: Highlight the A paragraph. Green check appears.]

Part A names the spread of Enlightenment ideas — natural rights, the right to resist when rights are taken away — and points to the Haitian Revolution as the payoff. Clean identification with a real example. Point earned. Honestly, this is the easiest point on the exam if you know your content — you just have to *name* something specific.

[VISUAL: Highlight the B paragraph. Green check.]

Part B is where the magic happens. The student doesn't just say "revolutionaries liked Enlightenment ideas." They show the ideas going *into* the Declaration of Independence, through Jefferson borrowing from Locke. That's the mechanism: an ideology picked up, written into a founding document, turned into a claim for independence and rights. How, not just what. Point earned.

[VISUAL: Highlight the C paragraph. Green check.]

Part C: creole revolutionaries in Latin America running into peninsular elites protecting their power and wealth in the colonies. Specific groups, specific stakes, a real mechanism of resistance. Three for three.

[VISUAL: Scoreboard — 3/3 in green.]

This answer isn't long. It's just *precise*. Every sentence does a job.

## 4. LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 4B: THE ONE THAT STINGS (3:00–4:45)

[VISUAL: Sample 4B on screen. Score starts at 0, ticks up as we go.]

Sample 4B is the painful one, because Parts A and C are genuinely good — and Part B lost its point on something the student clearly didn't realize.

Part A earned the point: Enlightenment ideas, natural rights, the right to overthrow a government that doesn't protect them. Fine.

[VISUAL: Highlight the C paragraph. Green check — 2 points.]

Part C earned it too: the French government meeting the Haitian Revolution with a large military presence in battle after battle, backing the upper-class slave owners to crush the uprising. A real challenge, explained with a real mechanism. Two points in the bank.

[VISUAL: Highlight the B paragraph. Red X.]

But Part B scored zero. The student wrote that revolutionary movements "used peaceful ideologies to gain support for the movement by making it safe" — built entirely around Gandhi in British India.

College Board gave it zero, for two reasons. First: Gandhi is outside the time period. The prompt says 1750 to 1900 — Gandhi's campaign is a twentieth-century story. An example from the wrong century doesn't count, no matter how well it's explained.

Second — the subtler one — the answer never actually explains the *ideology*. It says nonviolence made protesting feel safe, which grew support. But what belief system is being used, and how does it change a society? "Peaceful ideologies" is a label, not an ideology. Compare that to Sample 4A's Jefferson-and-Locke move: name the idea, show who used it, show what it built. 4B has none of that — just the word "peaceful" doing all the work.

[VISUAL: Scoreboard — 2/3, amber.]

So a strong student lost a point to two classic errors at once: the wrong century, and a label where an explanation belonged.

## 5. LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 4C: THE ONE-LINERS (4:45–5:30)

[VISUAL: Sample 4C on screen — visibly short.]

Sample 4C is three one-liners, and it shows you the minimum in action.

Part A earned the point — barely. "Enlightenment ideas such as liberty [and] equality." An identification. Done. One point.

[VISUAL: Highlight the B one-liner. Red X.]

Part B: "Revolutionary movements used ideologies to support and justify their action." Read it again — that just repeats the question back in different words. No ideology named, no one using it, no how. College Board called it "not an explanation." Zero.

[VISUAL: Highlight the C one-liner. Red X.]

Part C: "Revolutionary movements faced challenges from both internal and external forces." Same problem — a fancier way of saying "they were challenged." No mechanism, no example, nothing a reader can check. Zero.

[VISUAL: Scoreboard — 1/3, red.]

Final score: 1 out of 3. The student knew the content — they knew Enlightenment ideas mattered. But on the two *explain* parts, they gave labels instead of mechanisms, and labels earn nothing.

## 6. THE MISS PATTERN (5:30–6:15)

[VISUAL: Side-by-side: "Label" vs "Mechanism" — 4C's B line next to 4A's B line.]

The miss pattern on no-stimulus SAQs is the same every time: **students answer "explain" prompts with descriptions.**

When the prompt said *identify* — Part A — all three students earned the point, because naming a factor is a content check. But Parts B and C said *explain*, and that's where the points bled: 4C repeated the question back to itself, and 4B wrote a confident paragraph about an idea it never actually named, from a century outside the prompt.

The pattern is this: on explain parts, students reach for a memorized example — Gandhi, "internal and external forces," "peaceful ideologies" — and stop one step too early. They give you the label. They never show the *how*.

## 7. THE TAKEAWAY RULE (6:15–6:45)

[VISUAL: The rule on screen, one line, big text.]

Your rule, worth memorizing word for word:

**If your answer could be true of any revolution in any century, you haven't explained anything yet. Name the mechanism.**

"Faced challenges from internal and external forces" — true of literally every revolution ever. Zero points. "Peninsular elites in the Spanish colonies fought creole revolutionaries to protect their power and wealth" — true of one specific thing, with a how built in. Full point.

Before you move on from any explain part, ask yourself: did I say *how* it worked, and *who* did it? If not, you've written a label. Go back and add the mechanism.
