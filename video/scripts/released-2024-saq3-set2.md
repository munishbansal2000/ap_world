## COLD OPEN

[VISUAL: Title card — "WE GRADED THIS: 2024 SAQ3"]

Three students. Same three questions about the Mongol Empire. One got a perfect three, one a two, one a one.

And here's the uncomfortable part: the students who lost points didn't write nonsense. They named real things — gunpowder, the astrolabe. Real historical things. And they still got zero.

Today we're grading all three live, the way the actual graders did, so you can see exactly where points are won and lost.

## THE QUESTION

[VISUAL: The three question parts on screen, one at a time]

In 2024, this was Short Answer Question 3, and it was Mongols all the way down. Part A: identify one development from around 1200 to 1300 that helped the Mongol Empire get established. Part B: identify one development from that period that let the Mongols support trade and communication across Afro-Eurasia. Part C: explain one reason the Mongol khanates helped spread cultural or religious practices.

Part A is causes, Part B is tools, Part C is the only one that says "explain" — meaning you must give the how or the why, one sentence of mechanism. And note that parts A and B say "identify": name a specific, real development, with enough detail that a grader knows you mean it. Dropping an isolated term isn't a description.

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 1: 3 OUT OF 3

[VISUAL: Split screen — student answer on the left, rubric checklist on the right. Check marks animate in as each point is awarded.]

Let's start with the perfect paper. Part A: the student named the decline of the Song dynasty in China — specifying it had lost northern territory to the Jurchens — plus the weakening of the Abbasid Caliphate in the Middle East. Two specific, dated, accurate developments, each with real detail. Point.

Part B: the Yam system — the Mongol postal relay network — tied to the Silk Roads. A specific institution doing a specific job. Point.

Part C: religious tolerance let the Mongols put conquered peoples' scholars and artisans to work running the empire — adopting a writing script from conquered Turkic peoples, using Chinese silk-making to grow trade. A reason plus the mechanism. Point. Three for three.

Now watch the wheels come off in sample two.

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 2: 2 OUT OF 3

[VISUAL: Same split-screen layout. Part A gets a red X; parts B and C get check marks.]

Part A: the student wrote, and I'm quoting, "Gunpowder allowed the Mongol Empire to expand their power to different areas in Eurasia."

Sounds plausible — all real words. But the graders rejected it because it gets the history backwards. The Mongols built their empire in the 1200s on horseback cavalry, composite bows, and military organization. Gunpowder weapons came later, and the Mongols picked that technology up from China rather than riding it to power. The accuracy rule demands historically defensible content, and "gunpowder built the Mongol Empire" isn't. No point.

Part B: this same student wrote about the Pax Mongolica — Mongol-enforced peace making the Silk Road safer from robbery. Specific, accurate, on-task. Point.

Part C: religious tolerance encouraged different practices along the Silk Road and inside the khanates — that's the why. Point. Two out of three.

## LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 3: 1 OUT OF 3

[VISUAL: Same layout. Parts A and B get red X's; part C gets a check mark.]

Now the painful one. Part A: the student wrote, essentially, "exchange between states." That's the whole answer — no specific development, nothing a grader can hold onto. The official commentary called it out directly: that doesn't identify a specific development. Worse, exchange between states is arguably an *effect* of the Mongol Empire, but Part A asked what made the empire possible. Wrong part of the story, and too vague on top of it. No point.

Part B: the student offered "trade routes and new trade technology," with the astrolabe as the example. Two problems, both fatal. First, that's a category, not a development — like answering "food" when someone asks what you had for dinner. Second, the astrolabe, a navigation instrument for ocean voyaging, had basically nothing to do with how the Mongols supported trade, which ran on established overland routes. Real word. Wrong job. No point.

Part C: the student said the Mongols were tolerant, and tolerance kept the peace because subject peoples could keep practicing their religions — which let those religions spread. Short, but it gives the mechanism: tolerance leads to peace, peace lets practices survive and spread. Point. One out of three.

## THE MISS PATTERN

[VISUAL: Two-column graphic — "VAGUE" vs. "REAL BUT WRONG," with the failed answers listed under each.]

So what's the pattern? These misses are two versions of the same disease, and it's the most common way students bleed points on identify questions.

Version one is the vague answer. "Exchange between states." "Trade routes and new trade technology." These are categories, not developments. A development is a specific thing that happened: the Yam system. The decline of the Song. The Pax Mongolica. If your answer could describe a dozen different empires, it's not specific enough.

Version two is sneakier and more dangerous: the real-but-wrong answer. Gunpowder is real. The astrolabe is real. But gunpowder didn't build the Mongol Empire, and the astrolabe didn't run Mongol trade. That's what happens when you reach for a vocabulary word you remember from the unit instead of the specific answer the question asked for. Graders don't give credit for sounding historical — only for being historically right about the thing they asked.

And notice the cruel math: Parts A and B both said "identify," and students treat that as the easy verb. But identify is where all four lost points happened. The "explain" in Part C? All three students earned it, even the one-out-of-three paper, because explaining is just one sentence of how-or-why. The easy-looking verb is the dangerous one.

## THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE

[VISUAL: Full-screen text — "SPECIFIC. ACCURATE. ON-TASK."]

Here's your rule — three words to check before you move on: specific, accurate, on-task.

Specific: name the actual development, not the category. Accurate: the historical relationship must be really true, not just plausible-sounding. On-task: answer the part they asked — causes for A, trade tools for B, the why for C.

A point needs all three. Miss any one and you get the gunpowder treatment: a real word, and zero points.

## ON YOUR EXAM — MAY 2027

[VISUAL: Side-by-side — "2024 FORMAT" vs. "2027 FORMAT." 2024: "SAQ3 = no stimulus, choice of SAQ3/SAQ4." 2027: "Q1 = secondary text / Q2 = primary text / Q3 = map, chart, or image. No choice."]

One last thing for your exam. This question is from 2024, under the old format — back then, this was one of the no-stimulus questions, and students got to choose between two of them.

On your exam, May 2027, that choice is gone. You answer all three SAQs, and here's the big change: Q3 always comes with a non-text source — a map, a chart, or an image. Q1 is a secondary text, Q2 is a primary text, Q3 is visual. So this exact question — three bare Mongol questions with no stimulus — cannot show up on your exam as-is.

Picture it instead under a map of the Mongol khanates with trade routes drawn in. The map would practically hand you Part B — your job becomes reading the stimulus, not pulling everything from memory. The skills — identify the specific thing, explain the why — don't change. The input does. So when you practice, practice with maps.
