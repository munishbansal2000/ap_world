# We Graded This: 2023 SAQ 4 — The Question That Flips Its Arrow Three Times

**Target runtime:** ~7 minutes (~1,000 words of narration)
**Attribution:** Question from the 2023 AP World History exam, released by College Board.

---

## COLD OPEN

[VISUAL: "3/3" score badge next to one student response, "1/3" next to another — same question.]

This is the same question. Three parts, three points. One student got a perfect score — and one student got a single point. And neither of them ran out of facts. The student who lost two points actually wrote MORE than the perfect scorer.

So what happened? We pulled the official 2023 samples, graded them live against College Board's own scoring notes, and the pattern is kind of wild: this question flips its cause-and-effect arrow three times, and students keep answering part A when the question has moved on to part C.

Let's do it.

---

## THE QUESTION ON SCREEN

[VISUAL: the three prompt parts on screen, no stimulus.]

Question from the 2023 AP World History exam, released by College Board. Three parts, all about industrialization and the environment.

Part A asks you to identify one way environmental conditions helped industrialization get going in the 18th and early 19th centuries. Part B asks you to explain one way societies or governments reacted to industrialization in the 18th and 19th centuries. And Part C asks you to explain one way human activity changed the environment in the 20th century.

The skill being tested here is causation — the reasoning process College Board calls cause and effect. But notice the structure, because this is the whole trap: Part A says environment caused industry. Part B says industry caused reactions. Part C flips it completely — humans caused environmental change. Three different arrows. Keep up, because this is exactly where points go to die.

---

## LIVE GRADING: SAMPLE A — 3/3

[VISUAL: Sample A response, parts a, b, c. Rubric annotations highlighting earned points.]

First up, the perfect score. Part A: this student says Britain had abundant coal and running water — water wheels powered the textile mills, coal powered the steam engines — and that's why Britain industrialized first. That's a clear, historically defensible cause. Point.

Part B: rural workers left the cottage system and moved to cities for factory jobs. That's urbanization — a real reaction of society to industrialization. Point.

Part C: burning fossil fuels, especially automobiles, drove a huge rise in CO2 emissions. That's human activity causing an environmental change, named with a mechanism. Point. Three for three.

Notice what this response does: short, specific, and each part answers the arrow the question actually asked. Nothing fancy. Now watch what happens when you don't.

---

## LIVE GRADING: SAMPLE B — 2/3 (lost it in Part C)

[VISUAL: Sample B part c highlighted, with "WRONG ARROW" annotation.]

Sample B opens strong. Part A: Britain's waterways made it easy to move goods in and out of the country. College Board gives it the point — that's an environmental condition helping industrialization.

Part B: families moved to crowded cities hoping for higher wages, working long hours on different shifts that broke up the old family dynamic. Urbanization again — that's a real reaction. Point.

Then Part C. Quote: the student writes about how steamships and airplanes made it possible to move goods much faster.

And that's… not what Part C asks. Part C asks for an environmental CHANGE caused by human activity. This student never names one. Faster transport is an economic change, not an environmental one. College Board's own commentary is blunt: the response "focuses on the speed at which goods were transported but does not explain a change in the environment brought about by human activity."

Here's the painful part: this student clearly knew about steamships and industrial transport. They just answered the wrong arrow. They kept the industry arrow from Part A going — how industry got better — when Part C had already flipped to humans changing the environment. One missing sentence — pollution, emissions, something — and this was a perfect score. That's the most common single-point bleed on this type of question.

---

## LIVE GRADING: SAMPLE C — 1/3 (lost it in Parts A and B)

[VISUAL: Sample C parts a and b with annotation labels for each miss.]

Sample C is the cautionary tale, and it's worth seeing because the mistakes are so common.

Part A asks how environmental conditions helped START industrialization. This student instead writes about migration — people moving because places were too hot or too cold — and about trains, cars, planes, and even the telephone. None of that explains how the environment enabled industrialization to begin. College Board says weather wasn't a contributing factor and neither were later forms of transportation or communication. Wrong question answered. Point lost.

Part B asks how societies or governments REACTED to industrialization. This student writes that wealthier people made more products to make more money, and that industrialization grew some jobs and shrank farming jobs. Stop there and check the arrow: that's describing the EFFECTS of industrialization, not reactions TO it. Then the student adds that "certain people" were unhappy, splitting governments and societies into "split opinion."

That last bit feels like it might earn something, right? It doesn't. "Split opinion" names no actual action — no union, no reform, no policy, no movement. College Board's commentary notes the response never explains the way societies or governments actually reacted. Vague feelings about industrialization aren't a reaction. Point lost.

But Part C earns: the student says fossil fuels powering ships, planes, cars, and trains put pollution into the earth, and that the spread of pollution was rapid. Humans caused an environmental change, explained. Point.

## THE MISS PATTERN

[VISUAL: three arrows diagram — A: Environment → Industry, B: Industry → Reaction, C: Humans → Environment.]

So what did this question reveal? Two patterns, and they're both about arrows.

Pattern one: students answer the WRONG DIRECTION. Part A trained your brain on "things that helped industry." Then Part B needs "how people reacted" and Part C needs "how humans hurt the environment." Sample C's Part B described effects of industrialization — they were still running Part A's playbook. Sample B's Part C described faster transport — also still running Part A's playbook. In both cases the student had real knowledge and spent it on a question nobody asked.

Pattern two: for "explain," vague isn't enough. Sample C wrote about "split opinion" and unhappy people — no named action, no mechanism, no point. Compare that to Sample A's "widespread use of automobiles has resulted in a skyrocketing of CO2 emissions" — specific actor, specific action, specific environmental change. On a 40-minute SAQ, graders don't hunt for points. You hand them the sentence, or you don't get the point.

---

## THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE

[VISUAL: text card.]

Here it is, the rule for every causation SAQ: **read the arrow, both directions.** Before you write a single sentence, name what's causing and what's changing. Then check: is the thing I described as the RESULT actually the thing the question asked about? If Part C asks for an environmental change and your sentence ends with "faster trade," you've answered Part A again. Rewrite it.

## ON YOUR EXAM — MAY 2027 FORMAT BRIDGE

[VISUAL: 2023 format vs 2027 format side-by-side.]

Quick format note, because this matters for you. In 2023, this was a no-stimulus question, and students could CHOOSE between SAQ 3 and SAQ 4. That's gone. On your May 2027 exam, all three SAQs are required — no choice — and each one has a mandated source type: Q1 is a secondary text, Q2 is a primary text, and Q3 is ALWAYS a non-text source — a map, a chart, or an image.

So on your exam, there is no no-stimulus SAQ at all. If a question about industrialization and the environment showed up, it would appear as Q3, with a map or chart attached — maybe coal deposits across Europe, or industrial output by country — and you'd have to read the visual FIRST, then answer. Same causation skills, same arrows to check. Just expect to start from an image, not a blank page.

That's the question, graded. The arrows are the whole game. See you in the next one.
