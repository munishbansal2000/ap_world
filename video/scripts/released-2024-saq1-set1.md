## [0:00] COLD OPEN — HOOK

[VISUAL: three student scores appearing on screen: 3/3, 2/3, 1/3]

Here's the painful truth about this question. The student who scored a perfect 3 out of 3 wrote short, clumsy sentences with spelling errors. The student who scored 1 out of 3 quoted the passage almost word for word — and got zero on the part where they did it. This is a 2024 question about Hindu-Muslim cultural exchange in Mughal India, and it exposes the number-one way students bleed points on source-based short answers: they answer the right passage but the wrong question. Let's grade three real student responses together, and I'll show you exactly where each point was earned — and where it leaked away.

## [0:50] THE QUESTION ON SCREEN

[VISUAL: prompt text on screen; passage summarized as bullet points]

The setup is a passage from two historians making a case about Hindu-Muslim interaction in Mughal India. Their argument, roughly: the two communities mixed across art, court life, and culture — imperial painters borrowing from regional Rajput styles, mystical poets like Kabir and Guru Nanak drawing followers from both communities, Sufi scholars streaming in from Persia and Central Asia.

Then three tasks. Part A: identify one claim the authors make in the first paragraph. Part B: identify one piece of evidence the authors use to support their claims about cultural interaction — in the second paragraph. Part C: explain one reason Mughal rulers, around 1450 to 1750, would have encouraged these interactions.

## [1:40] WHAT EACH PART IS REALLY ASKING

[VISUAL: three-part rubric, one point each]

Three parts, three independent points. Parts A and B are a reading check: can you find what the author said, in the exact paragraph the question names? Notice the precision — (a) says the first paragraph, (b) says the second. That's not decoration. It's a condition of the point.

Part C shifts to your own historical knowledge, and the key verb is "explain" — how or why. And not just any why: the rulers' why. A motive belongs to the actor named in the question. Rulers encourage things for ruler reasons: order, loyalty, soldiers, taxes, acceptance of their rule.

## [2:40] LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE ONE: THE PERFECT SCORE

[VISUAL: Sample 1A response with rubric annotations]

First, the 3-out-of-3. Part A: the student wrote that Hindus and Muslims shared "different forms of art, social life, and cultural beliefs." That's a claim from paragraph one, restated in the student's own words. One point.

Part B: the student pointed to imperial court painters mixing with Rajput painters, plus the shared poetic and mystical traditions — evidence from paragraph two about cultural interaction. One point.

Part C: "to keep order and peace... to avoid conflict." Six plain words, and they name a ruler's motive — stability. One point. Three for three, in clumsy handwriting the grader ignored. These are first drafts under time pressure. The history was right, and every answer matched its task.

## [4:10] LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE TWO: THE 2 OUT OF 3

[VISUAL: Sample 1B response with rubric annotations; part (c) marked lost]

Now the student who lost one point. Part A: the Muslim population grew from 400,000 in 1200 to 50 million by 1800 — a claim straight from paragraph one, with the passage's own numbers. Point earned.

Part B: the poetry of Kabir and Guru Nanak, and how the mystics attracted followers from each other's communities — paragraph-two evidence about cultural interaction. Point earned.

Part C is where it breaks. The student wrote that rulers encouraged the interactions "because they had similar beliefs already and could share new ones in an effort to grow." Listen to what's missing: the rulers. That sentence explains why the interaction was easy — shared beliefs. But the question asked why the rulers encouraged it. The rulers didn't promote syncretism because it was convenient; the motive was keeping a mostly non-Muslim population from rebelling, winning acceptance of their rule, and drawing on the military and economic strength of their whole population. This student answered a different "why" than the one asked. No point. Two out of three — and the lost point had nothing to do with how much history this student knew.

## [5:40] LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE THREE: THE 1 OUT OF 3

[VISUAL: Sample 1C response with rubric annotations; parts (b) and (c) marked lost]

This is the one that hurts. Part A earned the point — Hindus and Muslims interacting across economics, politics, social life, and the arts. That's a paragraph-one claim. Fine.

Part B: the student wrote a long quotation about Muslim scholars arriving "in search of government jobs... or to spread belief." Sounds impressive. But College Board's own commentary is blunt: that evidence comes from the first paragraph, and the question asked for the second — and the quotation describes migration, not cultural interaction anyway. Wrong paragraph, wrong topic. No point.

Part C: the student said the rulers encouraged the interactions "to show evolution and progress in the lives of the people." That's not a historically defensible motive — rulers don't run cultural policy as a progress showcase. And the answer drifts off to people "living in Iran, Turkey and Central Asia," who weren't even Mughal subjects. No point. Final score: one out of three — from the student who wrote the most words.

## [6:55] THE MISS PATTERN

[VISUAL: three lost points, three one-line diagnoses]

Step back and look at the three points lost across these two responses. None of them was lost for lack of facts. Every one was a miss on aim.

First: the wrong paragraph. Part B names paragraph two explicitly. The 1-out-of-3 student gave paragraph-one evidence. Graders are literal — the address on the envelope matters.

Second: the wrong "why." Part C asks for the rulers' motive. The 2-out-of-3 student explained why interaction happened instead. If your answer doesn't name the actor's interest, it's not an explanation of the actor.

Third: the vague motive. "Evolution and progress" is a slogan, not a state interest. Rulers in 1450–1750 encouraged things for power reasons: order, loyalty, soldiers, taxes.

So the pattern: on source-based short answers, students don't bleed points on knowledge. They bleed them on aim — the wrong paragraph, the wrong actor, the wrong kind of reason.

## [7:40] THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE

[VISUAL: rule card on screen]

Here's your one rule: deliver to the address on the envelope. Before you write a single word, underline three things in the prompt — the paragraph it names, the actor it names, and the kind of answer it wants: a claim from the text, evidence from the text, or a motive from your own knowledge. Right facts at the wrong address score zero. Name the paragraph, name the actor, then write.

## [8:10] ON YOUR EXAM (MAY 2027) — FORMAT BRIDGE

[VISUAL: old format vs. new format side-by-side]

One last thing, because this is a 2024 question from the old format, where you chose which short answers to do. On your exam, in May 2027, there are three SAQs and you answer all three — no choice. Q1 uses a secondary text, exactly like the historians' passage you just saw. Q2 uses a primary text. Q3 uses a non-text source — a map, a chart, an image. The stimulus-free question is gone.

So the skill you just watched being graded — read a historian's claim, pull the right evidence from the right paragraph, then explain a motive from your own knowledge — is guaranteed to show up on your exam as Question 1. And now you know exactly where the points leak: wrong paragraph, wrong actor, vague reason. Read the address first.
