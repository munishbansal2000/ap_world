# We Graded This: 2024 SAQ 4 (Set 2) — The Date Line That Cost Two Points

**Target runtime:** ~7 minutes (~1,000 words of narration)
**Attribution:** Question from the 2024 AP World History exam, released by College Board.

---

## COLD OPEN

[VISUAL: "3/3" next to one response, "1/3" next to another — same question, same topic.]

This is the same question. Same three parts, same three points. One student walked away with a perfect score. Another walked away with one point — despite writing about the World Wars, the Cold War, Korea, and Vietnam.

They lost their points on the two smallest words in the question. Let's get into it.

---

## THE QUESTION ON SCREEN

[VISUAL: the three prompt parts on screen, no stimulus.]

This is a no-stimulus question about decolonization in Africa, and it has three parts.

Part A: one political factor, after 1945, that helped independent African states get established. Part B: one economic factor. Part C: one reason these new states adopted communist or socialist ideas.

The skills are identification and explanation. Parts A and B split one story into two lenses: political and economic. And all three parts are fenced in by a date and a place — "after 1945," "in Africa." Those fences are where the points live, and where they die.

---

## LIVE GRADING: SAMPLE 4A — 3/3

[VISUAL: Sample 4A response on screen, three labeled parts.]

First, the perfect score — and it has a surprise in it.

Part A: weakened European colonial powers after World War Two — huge losses in people and infrastructure — leaving them vulnerable to decolonization. Point.

Part B: the colonial powers were broke after the war and couldn't justify the cost of crushing rebellions. The people in the colonies saw it and pushed for independence. Point — economic this time.

Now pause. Did you catch it? Both answers are really the SAME fact: World War Two wrecked Europe. The student just aimed it through two different lenses — political weakness in Part A, economic weakness in Part B. College Board gave both points, because each answers the category it was asked. One historical development, two explanations — a legal move.

Part C: the Soviet Union pressured newly independent states — weak new countries at the height of the Cold War couldn't risk angering Moscow, so they adopted communist or socialist ideologies. Point. Three for three.

Short, specific, each part answered the exact category asked. Now — the contrast.

---

## LIVE GRADING: SAMPLE 4B — 2/3 (lost Part A)

[VISUAL: Sample 4B part A highlighted, with "WRONG PLACE / WRONG TIME" annotation.]

Sample 4B lost one point in Part A.

This student's Part A brings up the Axis Powers, "most importantly Germany and the Ottoman Empire," crippled by both world wars and unable to uphold colonial territories. The Ottoman Empire. On a question about post-1945 Africa.

College Board's commentary: this "inaccurately characterizes the relationship between Africa, Germany, and the Ottoman Empire in the period after 1945," conflating the two world wars and confusing interwar events with post-WWII conditions. The Ottomans dissolved after World War ONE — a grab-bag of "empires that collapsed" sprayed across the wrong century.

But watch what happens next. Part B: France, despite winning, paid such massive costs in the wars that it could no longer support its colonies. Point. Part C: unfair class structures and inherited Western capitalism pushed new African states toward socialist policies meant to redistribute wealth and stabilize their economies. Point.

Two of three. The painful part: this student KNEW the right move — France too broke to hold colonies is basically the perfect scorer's Part B. One sentence about the Ottoman Empire erased a point.

---

## LIVE GRADING: SAMPLE 4C — 1/3 (lost Parts A and C)

[VISUAL: Sample 4C on screen, parts A and C marked, part B checked.]

Sample 4C lost two points for two different fence violations.

Part A asks for a factor AFTER 1945. This student writes about the WWII conflicts themselves — smaller states resisting bigger ones during the war. College Board: the prompt says after 1945, and this response describes conflicts during the war. The date line is the whole fence, and the answer stepped over it. Point lost.

Part B earns: the bigger empires were left in debt from WWII and could no longer afford their African colonies. Point.

Then Part C. The question: why did postcolonial African states adopt communist or socialist ideologies? This student writes about Cold War conflict between the Soviet Union and the United States — then spends the explanation on proxy wars in Korea and Vietnam. The verdict in CB's commentary: the student "focuses on the impact of Cold War proxy wars in Korea and Vietnam rather than addressing postcolonial states in Africa."

The Cold War logic was right. But the question said AFRICA. Korea and Vietnam are not in Africa. The answer escaped the place fence, the same way Part A escaped the date fence. Twice, this student answered with the right topic but the wrong address — and scored one out of three.

---

## THE MISS PATTERN

[VISUAL: the prompt with "after 1945" and "in Africa" circled in red.]

The pattern: **answering the topic instead of the address.** Every point lost here came from drifting outside the fences the prompt built.

4B's Part A drifted out of the TIME fence — the Ottomans belong to a different century. 4C's Part A drifted out of the TIME fence too — World War Two is not "after 1945." And 4C's Part C drifted out of the PLACE fence — Korea and Vietnam are not Africa.

And here's the cruelest part: nobody lost a point for not knowing something. What these students didn't do was check their knowledge against the address on the envelope before mailing it. The grader isn't grading "decolonization." It's grading political-after-1945-Africa, economic-after-1945-Africa, and why-Africa-chose-socialism. Right topic, wrong address, no point.

---

## THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE

[VISUAL: text card.]

The rule for every SAQ you will ever write: **answer the address, not the topic.**

Before you write, find the fences — the date line, the place, the category — and check: does your answer live INSIDE every one of them? If your best fact is the Ottoman Empire, admire it, then throw it away — wrong century. If your Cold War evidence is Korea, throw it away — wrong continent.

And remember the perfect scorer: when the question splits one story into two categories, you can explain one historical development twice, once through each lens. One fact, two fences, two points. But each answer has to stay inside its own fence.

## ON YOUR EXAM — MAY 2027 FORMAT BRIDGE

[VISUAL: 2024 format vs 2027 format side-by-side.]

One format note. In 2024, this was a no-stimulus question, and students could CHOOSE between SAQ 3 and SAQ 4. Both of those things are gone.

On your May 2027 exam, all three SAQs are required — no choice — and each has a mandated source type: Q1 is a secondary text, Q2 is a primary text, and Q3 is ALWAYS a non-text source — a map, a chart, or an image. There is no no-stimulus SAQ on your exam at all.

On your exam, a decolonization question like this would appear as Q3, with something visual attached — maybe a map of African independence by decade — and you'd read the visual FIRST, then answer. The fence rule doesn't change. It gets louder, because the source itself becomes one more address to check your answers against.

Same fences, same lenses — just start from an image. See you in the next one.
