## [COLD OPEN]

**[VISUAL: Title card — "3 real student responses. 1 official rubric. We graded them."]**

The student who earned a perfect 3 on this question wrote a paragraph containing a claim College Board itself wouldn't defend. And the student who earned a 1 actually knew their history pretty well. So what separated them wasn't knowledge. It was one word.

"Twentieth."

Today I'm grading three real student responses to 2025 SAQ4 live against the official rubric — and the pattern of how points were lost will change how you read every SAQ on your exam.

## [THE QUESTION ON SCREEN]

**[VISUAL: Question displayed, three parts labeled A / B / C. Timer graphic: "~13 minutes per SAQ."]**

No document, no map — just the prompt. Three parts, three points:

A: "Identify one technological development in communication or transportation in the twentieth century."

B: "Explain one way nuclear technologies affected international relations in the second half of the twentieth century."

C: "Explain one way the spread of new technologies contributed to increased economic activity in the twentieth century."

## [WHAT EACH PART IS REALLY ASKING]

**[VISUAL: Parts A, B, C highlighted one at a time with skill labels appearing.]**

Part A is *identify* — name one thing. But College Board's rubric says describing means more than just dropping a term, so give it one characteristic. One solid sentence does it.

Parts B and C are *explain* — and that's the causation move. How or why. Notice both parts hand you the cause and ask you to draw the effect: nuclear technology → international relations. Spread of technology → economic activity. Your job is the connection in the middle, built on a real example. The example isn't decoration — it's the vehicle the explanation rides in.

## [LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 1]

**[VISUAL: Student response on screen, rubric checklist beside it: A ☐ B ☐ C ☐.]**

First response. Part A: the student names airplanes — "which allowed people to travel faster to farther places, as well as increase tourism between countries." That's a development plus a characteristic. Point. **[VISUAL: A ✓.]**

Part B: the student describes Cold War tensions, a nuclear arms race between the US and the USSR picking up after World War II, the constant threat of destruction, and both sides working to de-escalate. One detail in their Cuban Missile Crisis account is shaky — but the rubric treats responses as first drafts: small errors don't cost you as long as the content doing the work is accurate. The core — arms race, fear of annihilation, pressure to de-escalate — is accurate. That's what earns the point. Point. **[VISUAL: B ✓.]**

Part C: standardized shipping containers, moved by crane from ship to train, so more goods could be transported and sold around the world. That's technology → mechanism → economic effect. Point. **[VISUAL: C ✓.]**

Total: 3 out of 3. College Board agrees.

## [LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 2]

**[VISUAL: New response on screen, fresh checklist.]**

Second response. I'm going to grade backwards here, because this is where it gets interesting.

Part B: the student names Mutually Assured Destruction — the fear of nuclear war that kept the US and the Soviet Union from invading each other directly, and pushed them into proxy wars instead, destabilizing the Middle East and East Asia. Clean causal chain. Point. **[VISUAL: B ✓.]**

Part C: GPS making ship navigation easier, allowing greater mass maritime trade. Technology → mechanism → economic effect. Point. **[VISUAL: C ✓.]**

Part A: "the telephone, which allowed for faster communication." Sounds fine, right? Here's College Board's verdict: zero. The telephone's invention and early commercial use happened in the nineteenth century. The question said the twentieth. **[VISUAL: A ✗ — red stamp: "19th century."]**

Total: 2 out of 3 — and the point they lost was the easiest one on the page.

## [LIVE GRADING — SAMPLE 3]

**[VISUAL: New response on screen, fresh checklist.]**

Third response. Part A: the telegraph. Also a nineteenth-century invention. Zero. **[VISUAL: A ✗ — red stamp: "19th century."]**

Part B: the nuclear arms race — fear between the US and the Soviet Union, both building more and more weapons, producing the Cold War. Compressed, but the causal chain is there: fear → arms race → Cold War. Point — and notice how short an explanation can be and still earn full credit. **[VISUAL: B ✓.]**

Part C: the student writes that steam engines in boats made it faster and easier to transport goods for trade over water, increasing trade and economic activity. The mechanism is actually correct — it just belongs to the wrong century. Steam engines went into boats in the 1800s. Zero. **[VISUAL: C ✗ — red stamp: "19th century."]**

Total: 1 out of 3. College Board agrees.

## [THE MISS PATTERN]

**[VISUAL: Side-by-side of all three scores: 3/3, 2/3, 1/3. The lost points all stamped "19th century."]**

Look at what happened. Two out of three real students lost points the exact same way: they answered the technology question and ignored the date. Telephone, telegraph, steamboats — every one of those is a correct answer to "name a technological development." Every one is a wrong answer to "name a twentieth-century technological development."

And here's the painful nuance: College Board's own scoring note says a technology that *originated* in the late nineteenth century can still earn the point — if it was further developed, commercialized, or became widespread in the early twentieth century. Their examples: the radio and the automobile. So "it existed in the 1900s" isn't enough — your example has to *belong* to the century the question names. All three students reached for the most famous example they could think of — and fame is a nineteenth-century game here.

This is how students bleed on identification questions: they read the topic and skip the time frame. The date range isn't background decoration. It's part of the question.

## [THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE]

**[VISUAL: The rule appears on screen, plain text.]**

Here's the rule: before you write a single word, circle the dates. Every part, every time.

On an identify question especially, treat the time frame as a second question. When you reach for an example, ask: when did this become a real thing? If the answer is "the 1800s" and the question says twentieth century — reach for something else. Airplanes. The internet. Cell phones. GPS. Container shipping. Those all live where the question lives.

And remember Sample 1's lesson: you don't need a perfect paragraph. A shaky detail in the margins doesn't sink you if the core claim doing the work is accurate. Spend your minutes on the causal connection and the right century, not on polishing prose.

## [ON YOUR EXAM — MAY 2027]

**[VISUAL: "2025 format → 2027 format" graphic. Old: "SAQ4 — choose 1 of 2, no stimulus." New: "Q1 secondary text · Q2 primary text · Q3 map/chart/image. All required."]**

One important bridge: in 2025, students could *choose* between two no-stimulus questions — that's what this was. On your exam, May 2027, under the new format: no choice. All three SAQs are required — Q1 with a secondary text, Q2 with a primary text, and Q3 always with a non-text source: a map, chart, or image.

So this exact shape — a bare question with nothing attached — doesn't exist on your exam anymore. But if a question like this shows up as Q3, it'll be anchored to something visual: a chart of trade growth, a map of nuclear test sites, a photo of a container port. And parts B and C would still ask you to explain the connection — technology to its consequences. The grading logic you just watched would apply word for word.

And the periodization rule? That survives every format change. Circle the dates, every time.
