# They Were Right — and Got Zero: Grading the 2025 AP World SAQ3 (Set 2)

**Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes (~1,000 words of narration)

**Attribution:** Question from the 2025 AP World History exam, released by College Board.

---

[VISUAL: Close-up of a handwritten SAQ response. A red pen circles the word "Sepoy" and slashes a big red 0 across Part A.]

## HOOK

A student on the 2025 AP World exam wrote about gunpowder weapons in India. Every fact was real. College Board gave it a zero. Not because it was wrong — because it happened in the wrong century. The bolded date line was the entire question, and this student never really read it. Today we're grading three real student responses — one perfect, one that bled a point to the date line, and one that shows the other classic killer: answers that say nothing. Grab a pen. You're the grader.

[VISUAL: Title card. Then the full SAQ3 prompt appears on screen, three parts stacked.]

## THE QUESTION

[VISUAL: Each part highlights as it's read.]

Three parts, three points, no stimulus — and each point is earned independently, so a zero on Part A can't touch Part B. Part A says: "Identify one development that contributed to the spread of gunpowder technologies in the period before circa 1500." Parts B and C ask you to explain how military technologies affected land-based empires, and how navigational technologies contributed to economic change — both 1450 to 1750.

## WHAT EACH PART IS REALLY ASKING

Part A is a retrieval question wearing a disguise. The skill tested isn't "do you know something about gunpowder" — it's "can you stay inside a boundary." That phrase, before circa 1500, does all the work. Good answers: the Mongol conquests, warfare between states, Silk Road trade carrying the technology west from China. Anything outside the date window doesn't count — no matter how correct it is.

Parts B and C switch the verb to *explain*, which in College Board's language means showing the how or the why. "Gunpowder helped empires" is not an explanation. "Cannon knocked down city walls, which let the Ottomans take Constantinople and grow their empire" — that is. One names a topic. The other draws the line from cause, through the mechanism, to a specific result.

[VISUAL: Split screen — "A = IDENTIFY" on the left, "B & C = EXPLAIN" on the right. Scoreboard appears: "Sample 3A — ? / 3". The student's Part A text fades in.]

## LIVE GRADING, SAMPLE 1: THE PERFECT ANSWER

Let's grade 3A. Part A names the Mongol Empire — and gives the mechanism: quote — "The Mongol Empire spanned a large portion of Eurasia, which created safer and more uniform trade routes" — then connects it: safer trade meant gunpowder, which came out of China, spread across that network. Named cause, mechanism, specific result. One for one.

Part B: cannons knock down walls, letting empires overpower their neighbors — anchored to a specific case: quote — "Ottomans used gunpowder weapons to attack the city of Constantinople" — took it, renamed it Istanbul, made it their capital. Second point.

Part C: the compass let Europeans sail to the Americas, bringing raw materials into Europe and creating the mercantilist system — colonies trading only with the mother country. Third point. Three for three. Same formula every time: a named thing, the how-or-why link, a specific outcome.

[VISUAL: Scoreboard flips to 3/3. Transition: red "0" stamp. "Sample 3B — Part A".]

## LIVE GRADING, SAMPLE 2: RIGHT ANSWER, WRONG CENTURY

Now 3B, and watch Part A. The student writes, quote — "A development that contributed to the spread of gunpowder is the British East India Company attempting to supply their Indian troops with new guns" — then describes how that sparked the Sepoy Rebellion, because Hindu soldiers objected to the new weapons. The painful part: this is real — the Sepoy Rebellion of 1857 really did involve cartridge controversies. But it happened after 1500 — centuries after. The prompt said *before circa 1500*. Zero. The date line is a fence, and this answer stands on the wrong side of it.

But the student did NOT lose the whole question. Each point is earned independently. Part B named the Ottomans expanding with gunpowder weapons — point. Part C explained the compass carrying Europeans across the Atlantic, leading to transatlantic trade and the Columbian Exchange — point. Two out of three. The bleed stopped because the student answered what B and C actually asked.

[VISUAL: Scoreboard: 2/3. New sample. "Sample 3C — the vague answer".]

## LIVE GRADING, SAMPLE 3: THE ANSWER THAT SAYS NOTHING

Sample 3C is the other classic killer. Part A offers "paper money and commercial networks in Song China such as the Grand Canal" — related-sounding nouns, nothing that spread gunpowder technology. Zero. Part B is even thinner — quote — "weapons affecting the government and construction." That's not an explanation; it's the prompt's words rearranged. No named empire, no mechanism, no specific result. You could paste that sentence into any military-technology question in any century and it would fit equally badly. Zero.

And now the twist — Part C earned its point. The student said the compass helped discover new lands, which meant more land and more crops — but wrongly credited Christopher Columbus with the crop growth. College Board gave the point anyway: their scoring notes say responses are first drafts, and minor errors don't sink you as long as the real connection is there. The compass → new land → more production line was specific and defensible, so the point survived a factual wobble. That's genuinely good news: the grader is hunting for your correct connection, not hunting for a reason to fail you. Final score: one out of three.

[VISUAL: All three scoreboards side by side: 3/3, 2/3, 1/3. Two red circles: one around "before circa 1500", one around "weapons affecting the government and construction".]

## THE MISS PATTERN

What does this reveal about how students bleed points on no-stimulus SAQs? Two wounds, both about not answering the question that's actually printed.

Wound one: ignoring the boundaries. Sample 3B wrote the answer they knew — gunpowder in India — instead of the answer asked: gunpowder *before 1500*. Every SAQ part has boundary words: dates, regions, the verb itself. Students read the topic and start writing. Graders read the boundaries and stop reading.

Wound two: labels instead of mechanisms. On *explain* parts, students hand in topic labels — "weapons affecting the government" — when the point only exists for the how or the why. If your sentence could be swapped into a different question without changing a word, you've explained nothing. Name the cause, draw the line through the mechanism, land on a specific result.

[VISUAL: A single rule card, large text.]

## THE ONE TAKEAWAY RULE

The boundaries are the question; the mechanism is the answer. Read the date line like it's a command. And on every "explain" part, you owe the grader a named cause, the how-or-why link, and a specific result.

[VISUAL: "ON YOUR EXAM — MAY 2027" banner. Old format vs new format comparison graphic.]

## ON YOUR EXAM (MAY 2027): WHAT CHANGED

In 2025 you *chose* between two no-stimulus questions — SAQ3 or SAQ4. On your exam that choice is gone: all three SAQs are required, with the format fixed by source type. Q1 comes with a secondary text, Q2 with a primary text, and Q3 — this slot — *always* has a non-text source: a map, a chart, or an image. So this gunpowder question wouldn't appear naked in 2027; it might sit under a Silk Road trade map you'd be expected to use. The skills don't change — read the boundaries, explain the mechanism — but now there's a map or chart to pull evidence from. Read it like a date line: it's part of the question, not decoration.
